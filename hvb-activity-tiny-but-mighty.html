PART 1 · ~10 min

The Fastest Reproducer on Earth

Here's the setup: You just found out you have strep throat. The doctor says you've had it for about 48 hours. The Streptococcus bacteria causing it started from just a handful of cells. Now there are likely billions in your throat.

How is that even possible? The answer is binary fission — the fastest reproduction method in the living world. One cell copies its DNA and splits into two identical cells. Under the right conditions, that can happen every 20 minutes.

PART 2 · ~10 min

Your Body's Home Team

You have ~38 trillion bacteria living in and on your body — roughly the same number as your own human cells. Most of them live in your gut (your intestines). Together, they make up your microbiome.

And right now, in February 2026, scientists just made a major discovery about bacteria in your gut that most of us didn't even know existed.

📰 Current Event · February 2026 · University of Cambridge
Scientists Discover "Hidden" Gut Bacteria Linked to Good Health

Researchers at Cambridge analyzed over 11,000 human gut samples from people in 39 countries. They found a previously overlooked group of bacteria — called CAG-170 — that consistently showed up at high levels in healthy people and low levels in people with chronic diseases.

The twist? These bacteria are so hard to grow in a lab that scientists had basically ignored them for years. They're part of what researchers are calling the "hidden microbiome" — bacteria that exist in us but that we've barely started studying.

Source: da Silva et al., Cell Host & Microbe, February 2026

This is about you — literally

As a volleyball player, you've probably taken antibiotics for something at some point — strep, ear infection, whatever. Here's the thing: antibiotics don't just kill the bacteria making you sick. They hit your whole gut microbiome like a bomb. The good team takes heavy losses. That's why doctors say to eat yogurt (probiotics) when you're on antibiotics — you're trying to rebuild the roster.

Q5 The Cambridge study found "hidden" bacteria (CAG-170) that are hard to grow in a lab. Why might scientists have missed these bacteria for so long, even though they're inside almost every person on Earth? Answer Doc → Q5

Think about what it would take to study a bacterium. What tools and methods have scientists been using — and what's the limitation of only studying things you can grow in a lab?

If a bacterium won't grow in a petri dish, scientists couldn't study it the traditional way. The Cambridge team used genetic sequencing instead — reading DNA directly from samples without needing to grow anything.
Q6 Connect both parts of this activity: If your gut microbiome has ~38 trillion bacteria, and they reproduce by binary fission every 20–30 minutes — why does taking one course of antibiotics (a week or two) make such a big dent in your microbiome? Answer Doc → Q6

Use what you learned from the binary fission simulator. If bacteria can rebuild that fast, why doesn't the microbiome bounce back immediately?

Think about diversity vs. total numbers. Antibiotics wipe out certain species more than others. Even if the survivors multiply fast, the mix of species — the biodiversity — takes much longer to recover, because some bacteria are rarer or slower to reproduce.
