Session 1 · Part A

Warm-Up — From the Real Lab to the Virtual One

🎢 You already ran this experiment — with a real track, a real marble, and your own hands. The physics you observed there is exactly what this virtual lab is about. Now you'll control variables you couldn't control at the real lab bench: switch cart mass with a slider, crank friction up or down, and watch the numbers change in real time.

🧠 The Big Ideas Behind This Lab

⚖️ Balanced Forces → No Change

When all forces cancel out (net force = 0), the cart keeps doing exactly what it's doing. Steady speed on a flat track = balanced forces.

⚡ Unbalanced Forces → Acceleration

When forces don't cancel (net force ≠ 0), the cart speeds up, slows down, or turns. Dropping down a hill or friction slowing you down — both are unbalanced.

🌎 Gravity Does the Work on the Drop

Gravity pulls the cart downhill. Higher drop = more speed at the bottom. That's an unbalanced force causing acceleration.

🛑 Friction Fights Back

Friction always pushes against the direction of motion. More friction = faster deceleration. Less friction = the cart travels farther.

💡 Why does mass not change the speed? (open after you run it)

More mass means more gravity pulling the cart down — but it also means more resistance to acceleration (Newton's 2nd Law). These two effects cancel out perfectly. A heavier cart has more "engine" (gravity) but also more "load" (mass) — the ratio stays the same. That's why Galileo's famous experiment showed that a heavy and a light ball drop at the same rate.

Q 4

Mass — The Surprising Result

  1. Before running: What did you predict — faster, slower, or same? (Write in doc before launching.)
  2. After running: Was your prediction correct? What actually happened to the speed as mass increased?
  3. Newton's 2nd Law says: more mass = less acceleration with the same force. But gravity is also bigger on a heavier cart. Explain in your own words why these two effects cancel out.
  4. Even though speed is the same, does a heavier cart carry more or less momentum at the bottom? (Think: if you had to stop it with your hands, which one would be harder to stop?)
Momentum = mass × velocity. If velocity is the same but mass is bigger, momentum is bigger. That's why a heavy truck going 30 mph is harder to stop than a bicycle going 30 mph — same speed, very different mass.
