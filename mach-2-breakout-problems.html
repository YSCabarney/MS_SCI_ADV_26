Pair A · Inclined Plane
The Starship Problem
Simple machine: ramp (inclined plane) · MA = ramp length ÷ height · Effort force = Load ÷ MA
The scenario

SpaceX rolls its Starship rocket out to the launch pad using a massive transporter. Before the rocket gets upright, the transporter has to push it up a concrete ramp to reach the launch mount platform. The rocket and transporter together weigh about 5,000,000 N. The launch platform is 12 m above ground level. Engineers need to decide: build a steep short ramp, or a long gradual one?

📷 SpaceX Starship launch, Boca Chica TX — SpaceX / NASA (public domain)

Given data
Load weight5,000,000 N (rocket + transporter)
Platform height12 m above ground
Ramp option 160 m long (gradual)
Ramp option 220 m long (steep)
FormulaMA = ramp length ÷ height  ·  Effort = Load ÷ MA
Your tasks
1
Calculate the mechanical advantage of each ramp.
2
Calculate the effort force the transporter's engines need to push up each ramp. (Assume no friction for this ideal calculation.)
3
Look at the two effort forces you calculated. The gradual ramp needs less force — but it's also longer. Which ramp would you choose if you were the engineer, and why? Write at least two reasons.
4
Think about it: A simple machine like a ramp makes the force smaller — but does it make the job easier in terms of total work? What do you think happens to the amount of work when you use a longer ramp vs. a shorter one?
For task 1: MA = ramp length ÷ height. A bigger number means the ramp multiplies your force more.

For task 2: Effort = Load ÷ MA. The bigger the MA, the smaller the effort force needed.

For task 3: Compare the two effort forces. Which one is smaller? Why would an engineer care about keeping the force low even if the ramp is longer?

For task 4: Think about what W = F × d means. If force goes down but distance goes up, what happens to the product?
Ramp 1 MA60 ÷ 12 = 5
Ramp 2 MA20 ÷ 12 = 1.67
Ramp 1 effort5,000,000 ÷ 5 = 1,000,000 N
Ramp 2 effort5,000,000 ÷ 1.67 ≈ 2,994,000 N
Work — Ramp 11,000,000 N × 60 m = 60,000,000 J
Work — Ramp 22,994,000 N × 20 m = 59,880,000 J ≈ 60,000,000 J
Task 3 answerGradual ramp — 1,000,000 N is safer for the engines than 2,994,000 N; less risk of overload; easier to control the speed of such a heavy load on a gentler slope.
Task 4 insightBoth ramps require roughly the same total work (60,000,000 J) — machines never reduce total work, only trade force for distance. This is the core idea: a ramp makes the force smaller, but you push over a longer path.

Pair B · Lever
The Container Problem
Simple machine: lever · MA = Load ÷ Effort · A longer lever arm = more mechanical advantage
The scenario

When the Francis Scott Key Bridge in Baltimore collapsed in March 2024, divers and salvage crews spent months clearing the wreckage. One common tool for prying apart crushed steel sections is a giant Class 1 lever — a massive steel crowbar with the fulcrum set on a pile of rubble. A salvage diver needs to pry open a pinned shipping container door. The door requires 18,000 N of force to open. The diver can only push with 600 N.

📷 Crane barge Chesapeake 1000 at Key Bridge salvage, April 2024 — U.S. Army (public domain)

Given data
Load18,000 N — the force needed to open the door
Effort600 N — the most the diver can push with
Lever typeClass 1 — fulcrum sits between the effort and the load (like a seesaw)
FormulaMA = Load ÷ Effort
Your tasks
1
Use the formula to calculate the mechanical advantage the diver needs. What does that number mean in plain English?
2
A higher MA means the lever is multiplying the diver's force more. To get a higher MA on a Class 1 lever, the effort end of the bar needs to be longer relative to the load end. Does that make sense based on what you've seen with seesaws or crowbars? Explain why in your own words.
3
Think about it: The diver is 6 m underwater — she can only fit a crowbar about 8 m long in the space. Based on your answer to task 1, do you think a single crowbar will be enough to open this door? What could she change to make it work?
For task 1: MA = Load ÷ Effort. Plug in 18,000 and 600. The answer tells you how many times the lever needs to multiply the force — if MA = 5, it turns your 600 N into 3,000 N at the other end.

For task 2: Think of a seesaw. Where do you sit to lift someone heavier than you? The farther you are from the middle (the fulcrum), the more force advantage you have. A longer effort arm = more MA.

For task 3: An MA of 30 means the crowbar's effort side needs to be 30 times longer than the load side. If the load side is about 0.4 m, how long does the crowbar need to be total? Is that realistic in tight underwater space?
Task 1 — MA18,000 ÷ 600 = 30. The lever needs to multiply her force 30 times.
Task 2 conceptOn a Class 1 lever, the longer the effort arm relative to the load arm, the greater the MA. Like a seesaw — sitting farther from the middle lets you lift a heavier person.
Task 3 — fits?With MA = 30 and a load arm of ~0.4 m, the effort arm needs to be ~12 m → total bar ≈ 12.4 m. That does NOT fit in 8 m of space.
What she can doAdd a second diver to increase effort force (which reduces the MA needed). Use a pulley instead. Or try to crack the door seal first to reduce the 18,000 N load.

Pair C · Pulley
The Wind Turbine Problem
Simple machine: pulley · MA = Load ÷ Effort · More pulleys = more MA = less force needed
The scenario

The US reached a record 150 gigawatts of installed wind power in 2024. Installing one offshore wind turbine means lifting a single blade — which weighs about 120,000 N — straight up to a height of 80 m above the ship deck. The crane on the installation ship uses a pulley system to do this. Here's the problem: the crane's motor can only pull with 30,000 N of force — nowhere near enough to lift the blade directly.

📷 Wind turbine installation vessel TIV Resolution — Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)

Given data
Blade weight120,000 N (the load)
Lift height80 m
Motor max pull30,000 N (the effort)
FormulaMA = Load ÷ Effort
Key ideaAdding more pulleys increases MA — but the cable gets pulled through a longer distance
Your tasks
1
Use the formula to calculate the mechanical advantage the pulley system needs so the motor can lift the blade without being overloaded.
2
Here's the trade-off with pulleys: every time you double the MA, the motor has to pull twice as much cable. If the blade needs to go up 80 m, how many meters of cable does the motor actually have to pull through? (Hint: multiply the lift height by the MA.)
3
Think about it: The crane company wants to use fewer pulleys to save weight on the ship. If they only get an MA of 3 instead of 4, what effort force would the motor need? Can the motor handle it? What would the company need to change to make a 3-pulley system work?
For task 1: MA = Load ÷ Effort = 120,000 ÷ 30,000. What do you get? That's how many times the pulley system needs to multiply the motor's force.

For task 2: If the blade goes up 80 m and the MA is 4, the cable has to travel 4 × 80 m. That's the trade-off — less force means more cable distance.

For task 3: Plug MA = 3 back in: Effort = Load ÷ MA = 120,000 ÷ 3. Is that effort force above or below 30,000 N? If it's above, the motor can't do it alone. What could change?
Task 1 — MA120,000 ÷ 30,000 = 4. The pulley system needs to multiply the motor's force 4 times.
Task 2 — cable80 m × 4 = 320 m of cable pulled to raise the blade 80 m. Same trade-off as the ramp: less force, more distance.
Task 3 — MA of 3Effort = 120,000 ÷ 3 = 40,000 N — over the motor's 30,000 N limit. It won't work.
What to changeUpgrade to a stronger motor (40,000 N+), use two cranes sharing the load, or only use the lighter pulley system for smaller blades under 90,000 N.
