Motion Investigation Bureau — Case 06 · Partner Lab

The Return Trip

Strap a GPS tracker onto a runner. You and your partner control her speed on each leg of a route — then read the tracker's own numbers to figure out today's mystery.

Partners: 2 per breakout room Time: 15–20 min Prereq: Case 01–02, The Motion Files
The Big Question

Can a runner be moving — fast, the entire time — and still have an average velocity of zero? You're about to build a route and let the GPS tracker settle it.

Your Path Through This Activity (7 steps)
  1. Read the Big Question above — that's what this whole activity is trying to answer.
  2. Skim the Quick Reference below if the vocabulary feels rusty.
  3. Click your assigned Mission tab and read Why It Matters, then make your prediction.
  4. Scroll to the "Try This First" warm-up right above the sliders — do it with your partner before anything else.
  5. Read Your Goal for your mission, then experiment with the sliders until the pill says Mission Solved.
  6. Click Run Simulation to watch it play out, then write your Case Note together. One partner types; both talk it through.
  7. If you finish early, try the Stretch Challenge. Then get ready to share out back in the main room.
Quick Reference — Distance, Displacement, Speed, Velocity
Distance — the total ground actually covered. Always positive, never zero unless nothing moved.distance = Σ |v × t|
Displacement — the straight-line change from start to finish. Can be positive, negative, or zero.displacement = Σ (v × t)
Average Speed — how fast overall, no direction. Never negative.speed = distance ÷ time
Average Velocity — how fast overall, with direction. Can be negative.velocity = displacement ÷ time
MISSION 1

The Loop Home

Why it matters: track coaches run "there-and-back" drills constantly — suicides, shuttle runs, warm-up loops. The GPS tracker's numbers are how a coach knows an athlete really worked hard, even when she ends up back where she started.

Before you touch a slider: if the runner ends up back at the start flag, what do you predict for her average velocity?

No wrong answers here — you're about to test it for real.

Your Goal

  1. Design a route (2 or 3 legs) that ends within 1 meter of the start flag.
  2. Make sure the tracker still records at least 20 meters of total distance — a tiny wiggle back and forth won't count.
  3. When the pill below flips to Mission Solved, click Run to watch the replay, then write your Case Note.
MISSION 2

Straight Shot

Why it matters: not every drill is a loop. Sprint tests and point-A-to-point-B splits are pure one-direction motion, and coaches want to know exactly when speed and velocity are basically telling the same story.

What do you predict has to stay true about the runner's direction for her average speed and average velocity to (almost) match?

No wrong answers here — you're about to test it for real.

Your Goal

  1. Use two or more legs, but never let the runner reverse direction (don't mix a positive leg with a negative one).
  2. Get the tracker to record at least 15 meters of distance this way.
  3. When the pill below flips to Mission Solved, click Run, then compare the Avg. Speed and Avg. Velocity tiles — how close are they?
MISSION 3

Read the Flatline

Why it matters: physical therapists watch position-time graphs exactly like this one to see the precise moment an athlete rested during a drill versus when she was actually moving.

What do you predict the "resting" leg (velocity = 0) will look like on the position-time graph?

No wrong answers here — you're about to test it for real.

Your Goal

  1. Set Leg 1 to a positive velocity (heading out).
  2. Set Leg 2's velocity to exactly 0, with a duration of at least 2 seconds (resting).
  3. Set Leg 3 to a negative velocity (heading back), landing within 2 meters of the start.
  4. When the pill below flips to Mission Solved, click Run and study the flat middle section of the graph.

Try This First (with your partner — don't skip this)

Before you change anything, the tracker below is already mid-route. Look at it together.

  1. Find the Position–time graph in the "Live Readout" panel below. Does the line ever slope downward? What does a downward slope mean about which way the runner is moving?
  2. Find the Distance and Displacement tiles further down the page. Is one number bigger than the other? Talk through why that might be, using today's vocabulary.
  3. Now actually try it: drag Leg 2's velocity slider all the way to -8 and watch the Displacement tile. Does the runner end up closer to home, or farther away? Then drag it back wherever you'd like — you can't break anything.

Stretch Challenge (if you finish early)

Could you hit an average velocity of exactly 0 m/s using only ONE leg (not two or three)? Try it, then explain why it does or doesn't work.

Two different partner teams could solve Mission 2 with totally different slider numbers. What do ALL working solutions have in common, no matter the numbers?

Compare the slope right before the rest to the slope right after it. What changed, and what does that tell you about the runner's direction?

Stuck? Reveal a Detective's Hint
Try: Leg 1 velocity 5, duration 4 (that sends her +20 m). Leg 2 velocity -5, duration 4 (that's -20 m). Run it — how close to the flag did she land, and what's the total distance?
What happens to distance vs. displacement the instant the runner reverses direction, even for one leg? Try keeping Leg 1 and Leg 2 both positive, but different speeds, and see what the tiles do.
Set Leg 2's velocity slider to exactly 0 — but give Leg 2 a duration of 2 seconds or more. Then set Leg 1 positive and Leg 3 negative so she lands back near the flag.

Bring This Back to the Bureau

  1. Share your mission and the exact slider values that solved it.
  2. Was there a moment your speed and velocity readouts disagreed the most? What was the runner doing on the track at that instant?
  3. Case 01 claimed "slope of a position-time graph = speed." After today, how would you correct that claim now that direction is in the picture?

Every route above is legal — a real GPS tracker on a real athlete would report the exact same distance, displacement, speed, and velocity numbers you just saw.
