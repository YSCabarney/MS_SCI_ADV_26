Cell Size Warm-Up

How small is small? In this quick activity you will predict the sizes of cell parts, see how surprising the real numbers are, and use scale to make the tiny things human-sized. This is the thinking you will use in your lab prep.

Time: 10–15 minHow: whole class on video, answers in chat
STEP 1

Line them up

≈ 3 min

Predict: put these seven things in order from smallest (top) to largest (bottom). Use the arrows to move them. Then click Check my order.

    Sizes on a log scale (each step to the right is 10 times bigger):

    Notice

    A grain of salt is about 25,000 times wider than a ribosome. A plant cell (about 100 µm) is only 1/5 the width of a grain of salt.

    Talk about it: Which one surprised you most? Why is it hard to picture sizes this small?

    STEP 2

    Scale it up

    ≈ 5 min

    Scientists use scale to make tiny things easy to picture. Imagine blowing up a plant cell (100 µm wide) until it is as wide as the size you pick below. Everything inside grows by the same scale.

    scale = model size ÷ real size
    Cell partShapeJobReal sizeSize in the modelAbout as big as…
    Talk about it
    • Which part is the size of a beach ball? Which is the size of a grain of sand?
    • A cell has one nucleus but thousands of ribosomes. How could each part's size and job explain that?
    • Why is the chloroplast a flat disc and the mitochondrion a folded bean? (Hint: think surface area.)
    STEP 3

    Now you try it (in chat)

    ≈ 2 min

    A textbook draws a plant cell 10 cm wide. The real cell is 100 µm wide. (Remember: 1 cm = 10,000 µm.)

    1. What is the scale of the drawing?
    2. At that scale, how many mm wide would you draw a 1 µm mitochondrion?
    Check the answers

    10 cm = 100,000 µm. Scale = 100,000 ÷ 100 = 1,000×.

    1 µm × 1,000 = 1,000 µm = 1 mm.

    STEP 4

    Your lab prep starts now

    ≈ 2 min

    You just did the three big ideas of the lab: compare sizes, use scale, and connect each cell part's size, shape, and job. In the lab you will measure real objects with a digital microscope and ask:

    Our testable question

    Does the real size we calculate for a tiny feature stay the same when we change the microscope's magnification?

    In your Prep Assignment you will:

    • Research sizes (the "size ladder") and build an organelle fact sheet (job, shape, size).
    • Practice unit conversions and scale problems like the ones you just did.
    • Plan your variables, hypothesis, and objects.
    Make your copy of the Prep Assignment Open the Scale Lab page
    Exit ticket (type in chat)

    Finish this sentence: "One thing about cell sizes that surprised me is ___, and one question I have before the lab is ___."

    For the teacher: 12-minute plan and answers
    1. 0:00–0:03 Line them up. Screen-share this page. Have students call out or type an order in chat. Move cards for them, click Check my order, and discuss the surprises.
    2. 0:03–0:08 Scale it up. Start at 5 m (classroom). Click through the presets. Ask the three talk questions. Point out that the one nucleus vs. thousands of ribosomes idea links size to job, and that flat shapes (chloroplast) and folded shapes (mitochondrion) give more surface for their jobs.
    3. 0:08–0:10 Now you try it. Students answer in chat. Correct order of sizes: ribosome, mitochondrion, bacterium, red blood cell, plant cell, hair, salt grain (accept hair and plant cell swapped).
    4. 0:10–0:12 Bridge. Show the testable question, the prep assignment, and the exit ticket. Remind them the lab uses ideas from today.

    Sizes are typical approximate values: hair 70 µm, salt 500 µm, red blood cell 7.5 µm, plant cell 100 µm, mitochondrion 1 µm, ribosome 0.02 µm, bacterium 2 µm, nucleus 10 µm, chloroplast 5 µm, lysosome 0.5 µm, cell wall thickness 0.2 µm.
