Session 1 · before the lab

Plan your test

Testable question

"Does the amount of salt in the water change how much a potato strip's length changes?"

Hypothesis

"If I soak a potato strip in water with more salt, then its length will ______, because water will move ______ the potato cells by osmosis."

💡 Need a hint?

Water moves from where there's more water to where there's less water. Salty water has less water in it than the inside of a potato cell does. So which way will the water go?

Variables

In your Google Doc, write the independent variable, the dependent variable, and at least three controlled variables.

💡 Hint: Independent variable

The one thing that's different in each cup on purpose.

💡 Hint: Dependent variable

The number you measure before and after soaking.

💡 Hint: Controlled variables

Think about the potato, the strip size, the amount of water, the water temperature, and the soak time.

Materials

🔬 Microscope (charged) + Max-See app 🥔 3 potato strips (pre-cut, same size) 🥒 2 thin cucumber slices 🧂 Table salt + measuring spoons 🥤 3 clear cups, same amount of water 📏 Millimeter ruler 🧻 Paper towels + plate 🪧 Poster board + markers

Safety First

  • An adult cuts the potato and cucumber. Students don't use the knife.
  • Do not eat anything used in the lab.
  • Keep the microscope dry. Hold it above the cucumber, and never let the lens touch the wet slice or the salt.
  • Wipe up spills right away, because a wet table can damage electronics.
  • Wash your hands before and after. Throw the food away when you're done.

Part A · Salty Spuds

≈ 5 min setup + 5 min results
  1. Fill 3 cups with the same amount of room-temperature water (about 1 cup each). Label them 1, 2 and 3 with a marker.
  2. Add salt: Cup 1 gets none. Cup 2 gets 1 teaspoon. Cup 3 gets 2 tablespoons. Stir until the salt dissolves.
  3. Measure each strip's starting length against the ruler, to the nearest half-millimeter. Scale Lab skill: hold the microscope over the end of the strip with the ruler touching it in the same shot, and snap a photo.
  4. Floppy test: hold each strip by one end, level with the table. Score it: 1 = stiff (doesn't droop), 2 = bends a little, 3 = droops like a noodle.
  5. Drop all 3 strips in at the same time, and Player 3 starts the 25-minute Soak Timer. Then go on to Part B.
  6. When the timer ends: take the strips out, pat them dry, and measure and floppy-test them again. Record everything.

💡 What to expect

The length changes may be small, only 1 to 3 mm. That's real data, the same as in the Scale Lab, so don't "fix" it. The floppy test usually shows the difference more clearly. If a strip got floppier, it lost water. If it got stiffer, it gained water.

Part B · Sweaty Cucumber (Microscope)

≈ 10 min

The potato is slow, but the cucumber is fast. You're about to watch osmosis happen live.

  1. Lay a thin cucumber slice on a dry plate. Pat the top dry with a paper towel.
  2. With your finger, draw an imaginary line down the middle. Sprinkle a pinch of salt on the right half only. Leave the left half plain. The plain half is your control.
  3. Hold the microscope 3–60 mm above the slice so the line between the two halves is in the middle of the screen. Take a photo: 0 minutes.
  4. Wait. Take photos at 3 minutes and at 6 minutes, at the same zoom and the same spot.
  5. While you wait, zoom in on the plain half. Can you see the tiny boxes of the cucumber cells? Those are what Robert Hooke saw in cork!
💡 What should I be looking for?

Look for shiny beads of water appearing on the salted side. The salt is outside the cells, so there's less water outside than inside. Water slides downhill out of the cells by osmosis. Does anything like that happen on the plain side?

Real world: this is why cooks salt cucumbers and eggplant before cooking (to pull water out), and why a slug shrivels when salt touches it. It's also why you can't drink seawater: it would pull water out of your cells.

Homework

Analysis Questions

Answer these in complete sentences in your Google Doc:

  1. Which cup's strip changed the most? Use numbers from your data table as evidence.
  2. Where did the water go in Cup 3: into the potato cells or out of them? How do you know?
  3. What did you see on the salted half of the cucumber that you didn't see on the plain half? Why did you need the plain half?
  4. Was osmosis in this lab passive or active transport? Explain using the words concentration and energy.
  5. Was your hypothesis supported? Name one real source of error in your measurements.

Conclusion (one paragraph)

Restate the testable question in your own words, say what your data showed, explain whether your hypothesis was supported, and connect the result to one real-world example of osmosis.

Glossary

  • Osmosis: the diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane.
  • Concentration: how much of a material is packed into a space.
  • Control: the part of an experiment left unchanged so you have something to compare against (Cup 1, and the plain half of the cucumber).
  • Claim: a statement that answers the question and is backed by your data.
