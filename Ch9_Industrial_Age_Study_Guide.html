The Big Picture

The whole chapter in four sentences In the late 1800s the Industrial Revolution entered a second phase, driven by steel, electricity, and chemicals instead of just coal, iron, and textiles. New industrial powers (Belgium, Germany, France, the U.S.) caught up to Britain, and businesses grew into giant corporations and monopolies funded by selling stock. Millions of people moved into crowded cities, where science (especially germ theory) and reform slowly made life healthier. All this change shook up old ideas about women, education, religion, science, and art.

9.1The Industrial Revolution Spreads

Textbook pp. 298–304

I. The Second Phase (late 1800s–1900s)

  • New industrial powers showed up to compete with Britain.
  • Factories were powered by electricity, not just steam.
  • New products, especially steel (and chemicals).
  • Business got organized differently, into giant companies.
  • The economies of the whole Western world were transformed.
Honors-level thinking What made the second phase different from the first? The first phase was textiles, coal, iron, and steam, and it was mostly British. The second was steel, electricity, chemicals, and oil. It spread across many countries, it was organized by huge corporations, and it was run by companies that hired scientists (see Section IV). Think "era of science" rather than "era of tinkerers."

II. New Industrial Powers Emerge

  • Industry spread to Belgium first. William Cockerill opened factories there using British know-how.
  • Then to Germany, France, and the United States.
  • Why they caught up quickly: plenty of natural resources (coal, iron), capital to invest, and they could follow Britain's lead. They copied proven technology instead of inventing it from scratch.
  • How Britain tried to protect its lead: it made it illegal to export textile machines or for skilled mechanics to leave the country.
  • Did it work? No. People smuggled the ideas out. Samuel Slater memorized the designs of British textile machines and rebuilt them in the U.S.

III. Uneven Development

  • Some nations industrialized slowly, mainly in Eastern and Southern Europe (for example Russia, Austria-Hungary, Spain, and southern Italy).
  • Why: they lacked natural resources and capital, had little government support, and some rulers and social elites feared the changes industry would bring (they still relied on peasant farming).

IV. Technology Sparks Growth

  • By the 1880s companies were hiring chemists and engineers to develop new products and machines, which is why it became an "era of science."
  • The dynamo (Faraday) generated electricity. Without it there's no light bulb, no electric motors, and no electric streetcars. It's the reason electricity became the new power source.
  • Interchangeable parts and the assembly line (Henry Ford) made mass production cheap and fast.

V. The Rise of Big Business

  • New technology required lots of money, or capital.
  • To raise it, companies sold stock, which means shares of ownership, to investors.
  • A corporation is a business owned by many investors who buy shares of stock. Investors risk only the amount they invested.
  • Some grew into monopolies that controlled an entire industry by wiping out the competition:
    • Alfred Krupp (Germany): steel and weapons
    • John D. Rockefeller (U.S.): Standard Oil Company (oil)
    • Andrew Carnegie (U.S.): made steel affordable, with a massive operation in Pittsburgh, PA
  • A cartel is a group of companies that join together to control the production and price of a product.
  • Why monopolies are bad for consumers: with no competition, they can set high prices and push out smaller businesses.
Cause → Effect chain (great for short answers) New technology is expensive → companies need capital → they sell stock → corporations form → the biggest crush rivals → monopolies and cartels.

9.2The Rise of Cities

The problem

Cities exploded in size as people came for factory jobs. Human and animal waste was left in the streets, water was dirty, and people got sick and died from diseases like cholera and tuberculosis.

VI. Germ Theory: medicine catches up

Germ theory is the idea that infectious diseases are caused by certain microbes (germs).

PersonContributionIn plain English
Louis PasteurPasteurization: heating liquids to kill microbesSafe, clean milk. He also helped prove germ theory
Robert KochIdentified the bacteria that cause tuberculosis (and cholera)If you know the germ, you can work to prevent it
Joseph ListerAntiseptics to prevent infection during surgeryKeep wounds and tools clean
Florence NightingaleOpened the first nursing school (1860)Disgusted by filthy hospitals, she made cleanliness and nursing a profession
William MortonAnesthesia (ether)Surgery without agony

Making cities better

  • Urban renewal means fixing up and rebuilding poor parts of a city. The most extensive example was Paris in the 1850s (wide boulevards, parks, and new sewers).
  • Steel made skyscrapers possible, which is how cities like New York grew up.
  • Mutual aid societies were self-help groups that aided sick or injured workers.
  • Standard of living measures the quality and availability of necessities and comforts in a society. For most workers it slowly rose by the late 1800s.

Factory dangers: the Triangle Shirtwaist Fire (1911)

At the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory in New York City, mostly young immigrant women worked long hours sewing blouses. In 1911 a fire broke out (probably from a discarded cigarette or match in a scrap bin). Exit doors were locked, the fire escape collapsed, and there were few safety measures. 146 workers died.

Positive effects: public outrage led to new fire-safety and labor laws, including sprinklers, multiple unlocked exits, fire drills, extinguishers, and factory inspections. It also strengthened labor unions.

9.3Changing Attitudes and Beliefs

Textbook pp. 312–318 · the "Progressive" spirit of making progress

Women's roles

  • Cult of domesticity: the idea that women belonged at home and men belonged at work.
  • Goals of the women's movement: the right to vote (suffrage), to own property, fairer marriage and divorce laws, access to education, and temperance.
  • Why it faced opposition: many believed men were superior, that women were "too emotional" to vote, and that a woman's place was in the home, not in politics.
  • Women's suffrage means women's right to vote. In the U.S. it was won with the 19th Amendment (1920).
  • Elizabeth Cady Stanton organized the women's rights movement (with Susan B. Anthony).
  • Sojourner Truth was an African American abolitionist and suffragist.
  • Suffragists like Susan B. Anthony were arrested in their fight for equality.

Education

  • Governments created free public schools and made attendance compulsory, and they built secondary schools (high schools).
  • Teachers received training; science was added to the curriculum; colleges for women opened.
  • Why more kids went to school: schools got better, laws required it, and industry needed literate workers. Also, fewer children were needed on farms.

Science

  • John Dalton: modern atomic theory.
  • Dmitri Mendeleev: the periodic table.
  • Charles Darwin: On the Origin of Species (1859), the theory of evolution by natural selection ("survival of the fittest").
  • Why Darwin was controversial: his ideas challenged religious beliefs about creation. Others twisted them into Social Darwinism, applying "survival of the fittest" to people and nations, which was used to justify racism, imperialism, and ignoring the poor.
  • Racism: the unscientific belief that one racial group is superior to another.

Religion & reform

  • Despite challenges from science, Christianity remained a major force in Western society.
  • Social gospel: a movement that urged Christians to do social service, helping the less fortunate.
  • Salvation Army: a Christian charity movement founded by William and Catherine Booth to promote the social gospel.
  • Temperance movement: a campaign to limit or ban alcohol. It led to Prohibition under the 18th Amendment (no buying or selling alcohol).
Amendment trick 18 came first and took the drinks away. 19 came next and gave women the vote. Both took effect around 1920.

The Arts in the Industrial Age

Three movements. Know the definition and the examples for each.

Romanticism

Early-1800s style that appealed to emotion and imagination and celebrated nature (a reaction against cold reason and ugly factories).

  • William Wordsworth: poet of the power and beauty of nature
  • Victor Hugo: The Hunchback of Notre Dame recreated France's past
  • Ludwig van Beethoven: composer who combined classical forms with a stirring range of sound

Realism

Mid-to-late-1800s movement that tried to represent the world as it really was, harsh parts included.

  • Charles Dickens: portrayed slum dwellers and factory workers. His novel Oliver Twist exposed child labor, crime, and poverty
  • Victor Hugo (later): Les Misérables, about how hunger drove a good man to crime
  • Louis Daguerre: improved photography (daguerreotypes, 1839). The camera captured reality exactly

Impressionism

Late-1800s school of painting that tried to capture fleeting visual impressions of light and color.

  • Claude Monet: placed brushstrokes of color side by side and let the viewer's eye blend them
  • Vincent van Gogh: experimented with bold brushstrokes and dreamlike images (often called a post-Impressionist)
Quick way to remember the order Romanticism = "How does it make me feel?" → Realism = "Show it how it is." → Impressionism = "Show how it looked for one moment."

Short Answer & Essay Practice

Try answering out loud or on paper first, then open the model outline. Honors answers give a claim, specific evidence, and the "so what."

1. How did the Industrial Revolution spread in the late 1800s, and why could other nations catch up to Britain so quickly?
  • Claim: Industry spread from Britain to Belgium, then Germany, France, and the U.S.
  • Evidence: Britain banned exporting machines, but people smuggled out the know-how (Cockerill to Belgium, Slater to the U.S.). The newcomers had coal, iron, and capital.
  • So what: Latecomers could copy proven technology instead of inventing it, so they skipped Britain's trial and error. Germany and the U.S. eventually out-produced Britain in steel.
2. Why were some countries slow to industrialize? Give examples.
  • Eastern and Southern Europe (Russia, Austria-Hungary, Spain, southern Italy).
  • They lacked natural resources and capital, their governments gave little support, and elites feared that industry would upset the old social order built on peasant farming.
3. What was the dynamo's impact on the Industrial Revolution?
  • Faraday's dynamo generated electricity in large amounts, which made it a practical power source.
  • It powered factories, lighting (Edison's bulb), electric motors, streetcars, and communication.
  • It shifted the Industrial Revolution from steam power to electric power, which is a defining feature of the second phase.
4. How did the need for capital lead to new ways of organizing business?
  • New technology (steel mills, rail networks, power plants) cost more than any single owner had.
  • Companies sold stock, which created corporations owned by many investors with limited risk.
  • The biggest corporations formed monopolies and cartels (Rockefeller's Standard Oil, Krupp's steel), which raises the question of whether that power hurt consumers.
5. Explain what happened at the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory in 1911 and what positive effects came from it.
  • What: a fire in a NYC garment factory. The doors were locked and there were no real safety measures, and 146 workers (mostly young immigrant women) died.
  • Why so deadly: locked exits, a flimsy fire escape, crowded floors, and flammable fabric scraps.
  • Positive effects: new fire codes and labor laws (sprinklers, unlocked and multiple exits, fire drills, factory inspections) and stronger unions.
6. What were the goals of the women's movement, and why did it face opposition?
  • Goals: suffrage, property rights, fair marriage and divorce laws, education, and temperance.
  • Opposition: the cult of domesticity (women belong at home), the belief that men were superior, and claims that women were too emotional for politics.
  • Outcome: the 19th Amendment (1920) in the U.S.
7. What idea did Darwin propose, and why was it so controversial?
  • Evolution by natural selection, published in On the Origin of Species (1859).
  • It challenged religious teachings about creation.
  • It was misused as Social Darwinism to justify racism, imperialism, and ignoring the poor. Darwin wrote about species, but others applied "survival of the fittest" to human societies.
8. In your opinion, what was the most important invention of the late 19th / early 20th century? Explain.

There's no single right answer, but strong answers pick one, give 2–3 specific effects, and connect it to other inventions. Example: Electricity (the dynamo) powered the light bulb, telephone, and electric motors, let factories run anywhere and all night, and is still the backbone of modern life. Steel or the internal combustion engine are also strong choices.
