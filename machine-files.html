Look Back: What You Already Know

Every machine in this unit runs on ideas from The Motion Files, Force Face-Off, the Coaster Lab, and Newton's laws. Here's how the old ideas connect to the new ones.

From: Force Face-Off

Forces are measured in newtons (N)

A soccer ball weighs about 4 N; a volleyball about 3 N. Now: machines change how many newtons you need to push or pull.

From: The Motion Files

Distance = how far something moves

Now: work = force × distance. Machines let you use less force, but you have to move a longer distance.

From: Coaster Lab

Friction fights motion

Now: friction is why no machine is perfect. Some of your work always gets "lost" to friction. We'll measure how much with efficiency.

From: Newton's Laws

Balanced forces = steady motion

Now: when you pull a box up a ramp at a steady speed, the forces on it are balanced. Every rope you pull also pulls back on you (3rd Law).

Quick check: tap each question to see the answer

1. About how much does a soccer ball weigh in newtons? A volleyball?
A soccer ball is about 4 N and a volleyball is about 3 N. A quick rule: weight in newtons ≈ mass in kg × 10.
2. Team A pulls a cart with 70 N to the right. Team B pulls with 40 N to the left. What's the net force, and what happens?
70 N − 40 N = 30 N to the right. The forces are unbalanced, so the cart speeds up (accelerates) toward Team A. (Newton's 2nd Law.)
3. A worker pulls a crate up a ramp at a steady speed. Are the forces on the crate balanced or unbalanced?
Balanced! Its speed and direction aren't changing, so the net force is 0 N. (Newton's 1st Law: moving doesn't mean unbalanced.)
4. Which way does friction push?
Always opposite the direction of motion. It's why the coaster cart was slower at the last speed trap.
5. You walk 5 m across the gym and 5 m back. What's your distance? Your displacement?
Distance = 10 m (the whole path). Displacement = 0 m (you ended where you started). For work, we use the distance an object moves in the direction of the force.
6. When you push down on the floor to jump, what pushes you up?
The floor pushes back up on you just as hard (Newton's 3rd Law). In this unit, when you pull down on a rope, the rope pulls back up on your hands.

Big Ideas

08

The Pyramid Problem

How did people move 2,300 kg stones by hand?

An average block has a mass of about 2,300 kg, so it weighs about 23,000 N. That's about as heavy as a big pickup truck. One person can pull about 250 N for a long time. Lifting that stone straight up would take almost 100 people pulling on it at once, and they couldn't all fit around one block.

Egyptian workers didn't have engines, but they did have simple machines: ramps, levers, and sledges (big sleds). They also had a clever trick for friction.

The wet sand trick

An ancient Egyptian tomb painting shows a giant statue being dragged on a sled while one worker pours water on the sand in front of it. In 2014, physicists at the University of Amsterdam tested it: the right amount of water made the sand firmer and cut the pulling force about in half.

Read the University of Amsterdam report →

Same sled, same weight. Less friction means less pulling force.
Connect: This is the Friction Finish Line all over again. More friction = more force needed. Engineers still work to reduce friction today.
09

What Counts as Work?

When does a force actually do work?

In science, work has a special meaning: work happens when a force moves an object in the direction of the force.

Work = Force × Distance

W = F × d. Force in newtons (N) × distance in meters (m) = work in joules (J).

Example: you lift a 40 N gear bag 1.5 m onto a shelf. W = 40 N × 1.5 m = 60 J.

  • Work ✔ Lifting a volleyball cart, kicking a ball, pulling a sled.
  • No work ✘ Pushing hard on a wall that doesn't move (distance = 0).
  • No work ✘ Holding a heavy box perfectly still. Your arms get tired, but the box doesn't move, so no work is done on the box.

Power is how fast work gets done: power = work ÷ time, measured in watts (W). Running up the stairs takes the same work as walking, but more power.

No movement = no work, no matter how hard you push.
10

Ramps, Wedges & Screws

How does a slope make lifting easier?

Simple machines make work easier by changing the size or direction of a force. They never reduce the total work. Instead, they trade less force for more distance.

Same work either way. The ramp lets you use ¼ of the force, but you push 4 times as far. (Ideal ramp, no friction.)

Inclined plane

A flat sloped surface: ramps, slides, loading docks.

Ideal MA = length ÷ height

Wedge

Two ramps back-to-back that split or push apart: axe, knife, doorstop, your front teeth, a zipper.

Screw

A ramp wrapped around a cylinder: jar lids, bolts, spiral staircases, parking garage ramps.

11

Levers That Lift

Where should the fulcrum go?

A lever is a stiff bar that turns around a fixed point called the fulcrum. You push on one spot (the effort) to move something at another spot (the load). The longer your effort arm compared to the load arm, the less force you need.

Memory trick "F-L-E, 1-2-3": what's in the middle? Fulcrum = Class 1, Load = Class 2, Effort = Class 3.

Lever math

Ideal MA = effort arm ÷ load arm

Effort arm 3 m, load arm 1 m → MA = 3, so a 300 N load needs only 100 N of effort.

Class 3 levers have an MA less than 1: you push harder, but the load moves farther and faster. That's great for kicking and swinging!

12

Pulleys & Wheels

How do ropes and wheels multiply force?

A pulley is a wheel with a rope around it.

  • Fixed pulley (attached at the top): changes the direction of your force. You pull down to lift up. MA = 1. Example: a flagpole.
  • Movable pulley (attached to the load): two ropes share the weight. MA = 2.
  • Block and tackle (several pulleys): MA = the number of ropes holding up the load. Cranes use these.

Catch: with 4 ropes holding the load, you need ¼ of the force, but you must pull 4 times as much rope.

Wheel-and-axle

A big wheel attached to a small rod (the axle). The wheel and axle turn together. Examples: doorknobs, screwdrivers, steering wheels, bike pedals, and the crank that tightens a volleyball net.

Ideal MA = wheel radius ÷ axle radius.

Connect: Newton's 3rd Law. When you pull down on the rope, the rope pulls up on your hands just as hard. That's why you can feel the load "fighting back."
13

No Free Lunch: Mechanical Advantage & Efficiency

Can a machine save you work?

Mechanical advantage (MA) tells you how many times a machine multiplies your force: MA = load ÷ effort. MA of 4 means your 100 N can move a 400 N load.

But machines can't make extra work out of nothing. In a perfect machine, work in = work out. In real machines, friction turns some of your work into heat, so work out is always less than work in.

Efficiency = work out ÷ work in × 100%. It's a percent, like the ones you calculated in the Coaster Lab.

14

Compound Machines

What happens when machines team up?

A compound machine is two or more simple machines working together. A bicycle uses pedals and gears (wheel-and-axle), brake handles (levers), and screws holding it together. Scissors are two levers with wedge-shaped blades.

A Rube Goldberg machine uses a long chain of simple machines to do a simple job the hard way, like pouring cereal with 20 steps. Each step is Newton's laws in action: an unbalanced force starts something moving, and it hits the next thing.

A tower crane combines a lever (the long arm balanced by heavy counterweights) with a block-and-tackle pulley to lift steel beams to the top of skyscrapers.

15

Final Challenge: Lift the Load

Can you design a machine for a real job?

The gym is hosting a tournament. Your team needs to move a 900 N equipment cart (nets, poles, and balls) onto a stage 0.75 m high. The student doing the job can safely push or pull only 150 N.

Like real engineers, you'll work with criteria (what the design must do) and constraints (limits like space and safety). You'll choose a ramp, a pulley system, or a combination, do the math, and defend your design with evidence.

Simple machines: inclined plane, wedge, screw, lever, pulley, and wheel-and-axle. Every machine you use is built from these.

Engineers use simple machines every day

Construction cranes, car jacks, elevators, wheelchair ramps, staplers, can openers, and bikes are all built from simple machines. Engineers choose the machine that gives the right mechanical advantage for the job, then design to cut friction so less work is wasted.

Your Body Is a Machine

Why is your body full of class 3 levers?

They have an MA less than 1: your muscles pull harder than the force at your foot or hand. In return, your foot moves much farther in the same time, so it moves faster (remember speed = distance ÷ time). A faster foot means a faster shot. Also check out the volleyball net crank next time you set up: it's a wheel-and-axle that lets you pull the net tight.
