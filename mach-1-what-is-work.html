The big idea

Work — the physics version

In physics, work has one specific meaning: a force moves an object in the direction of the force. That's it. If the object doesn't move, no work was done — no matter how hard you tried.

Work happens when: (1) a force is applied and (2) the object moves in the direction of that force. Both conditions must be true at the same time.

✓ This IS work
🏋️Lifting a volleyball cart off the floor — force up, cart moves up
⚽Kicking a soccer ball — force forward, ball moves forward
🛝Pushing a crate up a ramp — force along the ramp, crate moves along the ramp
🪝Pulling a rope on a pulley — force down, load moves up
🏗️Every single step in the OK Go machine — force applied, something moved
✗ This is NOT work
🧱Pushing a wall that doesn't move — force applied, distance = 0
📦Holding a heavy box completely still — your arms ache, but the box doesn't move
🎒Carrying a backpack horizontally across the room — force is up, motion is sideways
🚶Walking on flat ground while holding something — same deal: upward force, sideways motion
Wait — if carrying a backpack isn't work, then what is walking? ▶
Your legs are doing work when they push off the ground (force forward, body moves forward). But the work your arms do holding the backpack up is zero — the bag isn't moving up or down, just forward. This feels wrong because your body gets tired either way. That's because your muscles are doing biological work internally (burning energy to stay contracted), but physics only counts when a force moves an object through a distance in the direction of the force.
What about the effort force going into a machine that doesn't move the load yet? ▶
No movement = no work. That's why a machine jammed by too much friction does nothing useful — your effort force is applied but the load force never moves the object. The input work is "spent" entirely on overcoming friction and nothing comes out the other side. This is exactly why efficiency matters: a 0% efficient machine is just a friction converter.
What if the force and motion are in opposite directions? ▶
That's negative work — and friction does it constantly. When you push a crate and friction pushes back, friction is doing negative work on the crate, removing energy from it. That energy turns into heat. This is why the coaster cart was slower at the bottom than a perfect energy-conservation model would predict: friction was doing negative work on it the whole way down.
The math

Calculating work

W = F × d

Work equals force times distance — but only the distance moved in the direction of the force.

W = work (joules, J) F = force (newtons, N) d = distance (meters, m) 1 J = 1 N × 1 m
Worked example 1 — lifting a gear bag
GivenA volleyball gear bag weighs 40 N. You lift it 1.5 m onto a shelf.
FormulaW = F × d
Plug inW = 40 N × 1.5 m
AnswerW = 60 J
Worked example 2 — pushing a crate up a ramp
GivenYou push with 150 N along a ramp that is 4 m long.
FormulaW = F × d
Plug inW = 150 N × 4 m
AnswerW = 600 J
NoteLifting that same crate straight up 1 m (if it weighs 600 N) also takes 600 J. The ramp didn't save work — it just spread it over more distance.
Now you try — Egyptian pyramid block
GivenA pyramid block weighs 23,000 N. Workers drag it 10 m along a ramp using a wet-sand sled. The effort force needed is 5,750 N (wet sand, from Case 08).
QuestionHow much work did the workers do dragging the sled those 10 m?
FormulaW = F × d = ? N × ? m = ? J
W = 5,750 N × 10 m = 57,500 J
That's 57,500 joules just to drag one block 10 meters. The Great Pyramid has 2.3 million blocks. The wet sand trick cut that number in half — which meant half the workers and roughly half the total work done by human effort.

What about power?

Power is how fast work gets done: Power = Work ÷ time, measured in watts (W). Running up stairs does the same work as walking, but in less time — so running takes more power. A 100-watt light bulb uses 100 joules every second. We'll calculate power in the Case 09 assignment.

The origin story

Who was Joule?

Every unit in physics is named after a real person who figured something out first. Here's the story behind the one you just used.

🧑‍🔬
James Prescott Joule
1818 – 1889  ·  Salford, England

Joule was the son of a brewer — his father made beer — and he spent most of his life running experiments in a lab he built inside his family's brewery. He was not a university professor. He was not wealthy. He was an obsessive measurer.

His big question: when you do work on something — when a force moves an object — where does that energy go? His answer, proven through years of careful experiments, is now called the law of conservation of energy: energy is never created or destroyed, only converted from one form to another.

His most famous experiment involved a bucket of water with a paddle wheel inside it. He attached falling weights to the paddle via a rope. As the weights fell, the paddle churned the water. He measured the temperature of the water before and after, extremely precisely, and found it went up. The mechanical work of the falling weights had been converted into heat in the water.

🌡️
Joule once spent his honeymoon measuring the temperature difference between the top and bottom of a waterfall near Chamonix, France. His wife waited at the bottom. This is a true story.
📏
He spent 35 years refining his measurements of what he called the "mechanical equivalent of heat." His final value in 1878 was within 1% of what modern instruments confirm today.
🍺
He ran most of his experiments in the family brewery. When his brother sold the building, Joule essentially lost his lab. He spent the rest of his life in a much smaller house trying to continue his work.

In 1889, the British Association for the Advancement of Science named the unit of energy and work after him. One joule (1 J) is the energy needed to apply 1 newton of force across 1 meter. Every time you calculate W = F × d and write "J" at the end, you're using his name.

Words for today

Vocabulary — Case 09

These words come directly from today's lesson. You need to know all of them before the word wall homework is due.

New · Case 09
Work
What happens when a force moves an object in the direction of the force. Work = force × distance.
No movement = no work, no matter how hard you push.
New · Case 09
Joule (J)
The unit of work and energy. 1 J = 1 N × 1 m. Named after James Prescott Joule.
Lifting a small apple 1 meter ≈ 1 J.
New · Case 09
Power
How fast work gets done. Power = work ÷ time, measured in watts (W).
Sprinting up stairs = more power than walking the same stairs.
New · Case 08–10
Simple Machine
A basic device that makes work easier by changing the size or direction of a force. There are six of them.
Ramp, wedge, screw, lever, pulley, wheel-and-axle.
New · Case 10
Effort Force
The force YOU put into a machine to make it work. Also called input force.
Your push on a crowbar handle.
New · Case 10
Load
The object the machine moves, or the force the machine puts out. Also called output force.
The crate sitting on the ramp you're pushing.
