Case 01

Position-Time Graphs

To describe motion you need a reference point: a fixed spot you measure from. Position is your location compared to that point.

  • Time goes on the x-axis (it's the independent variable; it ticks forward no matter what).
  • Position goes on the y-axis (dependent variable).
  • The slope (steepness) = speed. Steeper line → faster.
  • A flat line → the object is stopped (time passes, position doesn't change).
slope = rise ÷ run = change in position ÷ change in time

Speed, Velocity & Units

In everyday life people mix these up. In science, they are not the same.

SpeedVelocity
Question it answersHow fast?How fast and which way?
Formuladistance ÷ timedisplacement ÷ time
UsesDistance: the total path, curves includedDisplacement: straight-line start → finish, with direction
TypeScalar: size onlyVector: size + direction
Negative?NeverYes: the sign shows direction
🔁 Back where you started = zero velocity. If you end where you began, your displacement is 0, so your average velocity is 0, no matter how fast you went. A ball in a loop has a big distance but a tiny displacement, so its speed is much bigger than its velocity.
➖ Direction signs: pick one direction as positive. Moving the opposite way gives a negative velocity, even at the same speed. Example: +4 m/s downfield, −4 m/s back.

Case 04

Acceleration

Acceleration is the rate at which velocity changes. Speeding up, slowing down, and changing direction all count.

acceleration = (final velocity − initial velocity) ÷ timeUnits: m/s² (meters per second, per second)
  • Positive acceleration: speeding up (in the positive direction).
  • Negative acceleration (deceleration): slowing down.
  • Constant velocity → zero acceleration, even if the object is moving very fast.
  • Turning counts! Rounding a curve or going around a loop at a steady speed is still acceleration, because direction (and so velocity) is changing.
Worked example: A skier goes from 5 m/s to 17 m/s in 4 s.
a = (17 − 5) ÷ 4 = 12 ÷ 4 = 3 m/s².
Then she slows from 17 m/s to 2 m/s in 5 s: a = (2 − 17) ÷ 5 = −3 m/s². The negative sign means she's slowing down.

Real world: GPS trackers, sprint-start video, helmet impact sensors, and motion-capture cameras all measure speed, velocity, and acceleration to keep athletes safe and improve performance.

Case 07

Forces & Newton's Laws

A force is a push or a pull. Forces are measured in newtons (N). Earth pulls on a small apple with about 1 N, a volleyball about 3 N, a soccer ball about 4 N, and you about 500 N.

1st Law · Inertia

Things keep doing what they're doing

A still object stays still, and a moving object keeps moving the same way, unless a force changes it.

Ball rolls forward when the van brakes · tablecloth yanked fast, snacks stay put · seatbelts

2nd Law · Force & mass

Force makes things accelerate

Bigger force → more acceleration. Bigger mass → less acceleration.

Same kick: soccer ball (0.45 kg) flies, bowling ball (6 kg) barely moves

3rd Law · Action–reaction

Every push gets pushed back

When you push on something, it pushes back on you just as hard, in the opposite direction.

Jumping: you push down on the floor, the floor pushes you up · kicking a bowling ball hurts

Net force: balanced vs. unbalanced

The net force is what's left over after forces work against each other. Forces in opposite directions subtract.

BalancedUnbalanced
Net force0 NNot 0 N
What happensMotion does not change: still stays still, moving keeps the same speed and directionThe object accelerates: speeds up, slows down, or turns
ExampleBus cruising at a steady 55 mph; equal tug-of-war pulls40 N vs. 70 N tug-of-war → 30 N net, cart speeds up toward the 70 N team
⚠️ Trap: moving does not automatically mean unbalanced. An object moving at a steady speed in a straight line has balanced forces.
acceleration = net force ÷ massExample: a 30 N net force on a 15 kg cart → 2 m/s every second

Friction is a force that rubs against motion and always pushes opposite the direction of motion. More friction (grass, rough track) slows things faster; less friction (ice) lets them travel farther.

Lab Files

The Coaster Lab & Friction Finish Line

Variables

TypeMeaningIn the Coaster Lab
IndependentWhat you change on purposeRelease height (cm)
DependentWhat you measureSpeed in the speed trap; whether the ball completes the loop
ControlledWhat you keep the sameSame ball, track layout, loop size, release method, speed-trap length, timer

Change only one variable at a time. If you changed the height and the ball, you couldn't tell which one caused the speed change. Doing 3 trials makes your data more trustworthy.

What the data show

  • Higher release → faster at the bottom, but the graph curves and levels off. Gravity speeds the ball up longer on a taller drop, while friction takes a bigger share at higher speeds.
  • Gravity is the unbalanced force that speeds the cart up on a drop. That's why the first drop is the tallest.
  • Friction slows the ball, so it's slower at the final speed trap than the first. More friction = shorter stopping distance (that's how coaster brakes work).
  • Loops: at the top, gravity pulls the cart down. If it's going too slowly, it falls off. Taller drop → more speed → loop completed.
  • Mass surprise: a 10 kg, 20 kg, and 40 kg cart dropped from the same height reach the same speed. More mass means more gravity pulling, but also more inertia resisting; the two effects cancel.
  • Momentum = mass × velocity. Same speed but more mass means more momentum, so it's harder to stop.

Lab math

speed = speed-trap length (m) ÷ average time (s)
percent change = (final − start) ÷ start × 100A negative answer means the ball slowed down.
Worked example: Start speed 2.5 m/s, final speed 2.0 m/s.
Change = 2.0 − 2.5 = −0.5 m/s → (−0.5 ÷ 2.5) × 100 = −20%. The ball lost 20% of its speed, mostly to friction.

Writing Strong Answers

You'll have two written-response questions worth 4 points each.

  • Show your math: formula → numbers → answer with units.
  • Use the vocabulary: net force, balanced/unbalanced, inertia, displacement, vector.
  • Name the law and say what it explains: "Newton's 1st Law (inertia) explains…"
  • Claim–Evidence–Reasoning: Claim = your answer in 1–2 sentences. Evidence = specific numbers from data. Reasoning = the science that connects them.
Case Closed?

Practice: Test Yourself

Work each one out first, then tap to check.

1. A walker's position-time graph goes in a straight line from 0 m at 0 s to 50 m at 10 s. What is her speed?

Slope = 50 m ÷ 10 s = 5 m/s.

2. A swimmer swims one length of a 50 m pool and back (100 m total) in 80 s. Find her average speed and average velocity.

Speed = 100 ÷ 80 = 1.25 m/s. She ends where she started, so displacement = 0 and velocity = 0 m/s.

3. A car moves at 25 m/s. About how many mph is that?

25 × 2.24 ≈ 56 mph.

4. A skateboarder speeds up from 2 m/s to 10 m/s in 4 s. What is the acceleration?

(10 − 2) ÷ 4 = 2 m/s².

5. A ball rolling at 8 m/s stops in 2 s. What is its acceleration, and what does the sign mean?

(0 − 8) ÷ 2 = −4 m/s². The negative sign means it is slowing down.

6. Two teams each pull a sled with 40 N in opposite directions. What is the net force, and what happens?

40 − 40 = 0 N. The forces are balanced, so the sled's motion doesn't change.

7. Which of Newton's laws explains why you should wear a seatbelt?

1st Law (inertia). When the car stops suddenly, your body keeps moving forward until a force (the seatbelt) stops it.

8. A ball crosses a 0.60 m speed trap in an average time of 0.40 s. What is its speed?

0.60 ÷ 0.40 = 1.5 m/s.

9. A ball's speed drops from 3.0 m/s to 2.4 m/s. What is the percent change?

(2.4 − 3.0) ÷ 3.0 × 100 = −20%.

10. A truck and a bicycle both go 30 mph. Which is harder to stop, and why?

The truck: same speed, much more mass, so it has much more momentum (mass × velocity).

11. A student tests whether loop size affects whether the ball makes it around. Name the independent variable, the dependent variable, and two controlled variables.

IV: loop diameter. DV: whether the ball completes the loop. Controlled: release height, type of ball, release method, track layout (any two).
