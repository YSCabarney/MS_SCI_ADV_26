Top Mistakes — Don't Give Away Points

❌ Velocity = Speed
Speed uses distance. Velocity uses displacement. If a runner finishes where she started, velocity = 0. Speed is never 0 if she was moving.
❌ Moving = Unbalanced
A bus moving at steady 55 mph has balanced forces. Motion doesn't mean unbalanced — change in motion does.
❌ Negative accel = going backward
Negative acceleration just means slowing down (or accelerating opposite to positive direction). The object can still be moving forward.
❌ Heavier = faster coaster
In the lab, all three cart masses hit the same speed. More mass = more gravity pull, but also more resistance to acceleration. They cancel.
❌ Slope = Position
On a position-time graph, slope = speed. A steep line = fast. A flat line = stopped. The y-axis shows position, not the slope.
❌ 3rd Law cancels forces
Action and reaction act on different objects. That's why they don't cancel. If forces are on the same object, then they can balance.

Position-Time Graphs — What the Line Tells You

What the line looks likeWhat it means
Steep upward slopeMoving fast in the positive direction
Gentle upward slopeMoving slowly in the positive direction
Perfectly flat / horizontalStopped — position isn't changing
Downward slopeMoving in the negative direction (going back)
Key fact: Slope of a position-time graph = speed. Calculate it: slope = rise ÷ run = (change in position) ÷ (change in time).
The hiker graph goes from 0 m at t=0 to 12 m at t=4 s. Slope = 12 ÷ 4 = 3 m/s. At t=2 s, the position is exactly 6 m (halfway). If the graph went flat after t=4, the hiker stopped.

Speed vs. Velocity — Not the Same Thing

BIG IDEA
SPEED — scalar
Uses distance (total path length)
Never negative
No direction
speed = distance ÷ time
VELOCITY — vector
Uses displacement (start → finish, straight line)
Can be negative (shows direction)
Direction matters
velocity = displacement ÷ time
The 400m runner example (this will be on the test):
A runner finishes one full lap in 80 s and ends up back at the start.
→ Average speed = 400 m ÷ 80 s = 5 m/s
→ Displacement = 0 m (started and ended at same point)
→ Average velocity = 0 m ÷ 80 s = 0 m/s
Same run, two completely different answers.
Soccer player example: Dribbles 20 m downfield in 5 s (+), then 20 m back in 5 s (−).
First 5 s: velocity = +20 m ÷ 5 s = +4 m/s
Second 5 s: velocity = −20 m ÷ 5 s = −4 m/s (same speed, opposite direction)
Scalars have only a size (magnitude): distance, speed, mass, time.
Vectors have size AND direction: displacement, velocity, force, acceleration.
When a problem says "which are vectors?" — look for displacement and velocity.

Acceleration — Speeding Up, Slowing Down, or Turning

CALCULATE THIS
a = (vf − vi) ÷ t
vf = final velocity · vi = initial velocity · t = time · answer in m/s²
🚀
Positive acceleration
Speeding up. vf > vi.
🛑
Negative acceleration
Slowing down. vf < vi. Still moving forward.
➡️
Zero acceleration
Constant velocity. Speed and direction unchanged.
1
Sprinter from rest: starts at 0 m/s, reaches 8 m/s in 2 s
a = (8 − 0) ÷ 2 = 4 m/s²
2
Cyclist braking: 12 m/s → 0 m/s in 4 s
a = (0 − 12) ÷ 4 = −3 m/s² (negative = slowing down)
3
Changing direction: a runner rounding a curve at constant speed is still accelerating because direction changes = velocity changes.
Yes. Acceleration = any change in velocity. Velocity includes direction. A car going around a roundabout at constant 30 mph is accelerating because its direction keeps changing, even though its speed stays the same.

Newton's Three Laws — Know All Three Cold

ALL ON TEST
1st Law — Law of Inertia
"Things keep doing what they're doing until a force changes it."
A still object stays still. A moving object keeps moving at the same speed and direction — forever — unless an unbalanced force acts on it.

Inertia is the tendency to resist change. More mass = more inertia.

Exam trap: The team bus moving at steady 55 mph → forces are balanced (net force = 0). Moving doesn't mean unbalanced.
2nd Law — F = ma
"A bigger force makes things speed up more. A bigger mass makes things speed up less."
F = m × a  →  a = F ÷ m  →  m = F ÷ a
Force in Newtons · mass in kg · acceleration in m/s²

A hard volleyball spike → more acceleration. A heavier ball with the same spike → less acceleration. Same idea.
3rd Law — Action-Reaction
"Every push gets pushed back just as hard."
For every action force, there is an equal and opposite reaction force — on a different object.

You push down on the floor → floor pushes you up → you jump.
You kick a ball → ball pushes back on your foot with equal force.

Why don't they cancel? Because they act on different objects. Forces only cancel when they act on the same object.
⚡

Balanced vs. Unbalanced Forces

BIG IDEA
BALANCED — net force = 0
Forces cancel out.
Motion does not change.
Still object stays still.
Moving object keeps moving same speed, same direction.

Examples: bus at steady speed, book sitting on a table
UNBALANCED — net force ≠ 0
Forces don't cancel.
Object accelerates: speeds up, slows down, or turns.

Examples: cart dropping off the first hill, friction slowing down a rolling ball
Net force = the force left over after forces work against each other.
Tug-of-War: Team Left pulls 30 N, Team Right pulls 50 N.
Net force = 50 − 30 = 20 N to the right → cart accelerates right.
Friction always pushes opposite to the direction of motion. It's always unbalancing or helping to balance forces. When friction exactly equals the applied force, net force = 0 and motion stays constant. When friction is bigger, the object decelerates.

The Three Lab Results — and the Physics Behind Each

KNOW ALL THREE
1. Higher drop → faster cart at the bottom
Gravity is the engine. The higher the release point, the longer gravity pulls the cart downhill, so it reaches a higher speed at the bottom.

Force connection: Gravity is an unbalanced force on the way down → causes acceleration → more height = more time accelerating = more speed.

Graph shape: Speed goes up as height increases — but the increases get smaller at greater heights (the relationship levels off; it doesn't keep going up at the same rate forever).
2. More friction → shorter stopping distance
Friction pushes against motion — always. More friction = bigger opposing force = faster deceleration = cart stops sooner.

Newton's Law connection: 1st Law — without friction, the cart would keep rolling forever (no unbalanced force). Friction creates the unbalanced force that stops it.

Real world: Roller coaster brakes at the end of a ride work by applying high friction — deliberately — to stop the cart safely.
3. Mass had no effect on speed at the bottom (surprising result!)
The 10 kg, 20 kg, and 40 kg carts all reached the same speed from the same drop height.

Why? More mass = more gravity pulling the cart down. But more mass also = more resistance to acceleration (Newton's 2nd Law). These two effects cancel perfectly. It's the same reason a heavy ball and a light ball drop at the same rate when dropped from the same height.

BUT: Even though speed is the same, the heavier cart has more momentum (mass × velocity). It's harder to stop.

Claim–Evidence–Reasoning — How to Write a Lab Answer

WRITTEN Q
CLAIM
One sentence answering the question directly. No "I think" — just state it.
EVIDENCE
Specific data from your lab. Numbers. What happened at each setting.
REASONING
Connect the evidence to the science. Name the force or law. Explain why it happened.
Example: "How does release height affect speed at the bottom?"

Claim: As release height increases, the speed at the bottom of the ramp increases.
Evidence: At 20 cm the speed was 1.3 m/s; at 30 cm, 1.6 m/s; at 40 cm, 1.9 m/s; at 50 cm, 2.1 m/s.
Reasoning: Gravity is an unbalanced force that accelerates the ball down the ramp. A higher release point gives gravity more time and distance to act on the ball, so it reaches a higher speed by the bottom.
🔄

Why the Loop Needs Enough Speed

At the top of a loop, gravity is pulling the cart down — which is toward the center of the loop. If the cart is going too slowly, gravity wins and the cart falls off the track. It needs enough speed so the centripetal force keeps it pressed against the rails all the way around.

That's why the low drop height (2 m) doesn't complete the loop, but medium (5 m) and high (8 m) do — the cart needs a minimum speed at the top to stay on track.
