📰 In the News — October 2025

NASA Found a Bacterium That Plays Dead — and Maybe Sent It to Mars

Breaking Science

The Bacterium That Fooled NASA for 18 Years

When engineers built NASA's Phoenix Mars lander in 2007, they did it inside one of the cleanest rooms on Earth — a place blasted with UV lights, biocidal chemicals, and dry heat designed to wipe out every living thing. They thought it worked. They were wrong.

Scientists later found a brand-new bacterium hiding in that clean room: Tersicoccus phoenicis (ter-SIH-coe-cus FEE-nix-iss). Even stranger, they found the exact same unknown species in a second clean room 2,500 miles away in South America. Nobody could explain how it survived — until now.

"It is not dead. It was playing dead. It is only dormant."
— Madhan Tirumalai, microbiologist, University of Houston (2025)

In 2025, researchers at the University of Houston published the answer: T. phoenicis can shut its entire metabolism down — entering a state so deep that standard tests read it as dead. Standard sterilization checks look for signs of life. If the bacterium shows none, it passes inspection. But it was just waiting.

When scientists added a specific protein called Rpf (found on human skin), the bacteria immediately woke up and started growing. Here is what makes this really unsettling: the Phoenix lander was launched to Mars in 2007. If any of these dormant bacteria were clinging to it, they may have landed on Mars — meaning we might have accidentally contaminated the planet we were trying to study for alien life.

Why this matters beyond space: The same survival trick could be happening in hospital clean rooms, food processing plants, and pharmaceutical labs right now — in bacteria we haven't detected yet. Scientists aren't sure if any of these hidden bacteria are dangerous, but as one researcher said: "What's unknown is scarier than what's known."

Source: Robin George Andrews, National Geographic, October 24, 2025

Connect to this unit: This story is a perfect preview of everything we'll study. T. phoenicis is a prokaryote (Stop 1). It survived because of adaptations that evolved under pressure (Stop 3). And the reason scientists worry about it reaching Mars comes down to one fact: bacteria can survive in conditions that would kill almost anything else. That's what makes them powerful — and sometimes dangerous.
Before we start

You already know more than you think

Remember our cells units? A bacterium is one single cell, so everything you learned about cell parts and cell transport is about to come back. Here's how:

From: Cell Theory

All living things are made of cells

A bacterium is a whole living thing made of just one cell. It eats, grows, and reproduces all by itself.

From: Organelles

Bacteria have no nucleus

Their DNA floats in the cytoplasm. They still have a cell membrane, ribosomes, and a tough cell wall, like the plant cells we studied.

From: Cell Transport

Osmosis can pop a bacterium

Penicillin breaks the cell wall. Water rushes in by osmosis and the cell bursts. Some superbugs use active transport pumps to push medicine back out!

Fun fact

Your mitochondria used to be bacteria

Scientists think that over a billion years ago, a bacterium moved into a bigger cell and never left. Today we call it the mitochondrion.

Big Ideas

1

Meet the Microbes — What are bacteria, and how are they different from our cells?

Bacteria are tiny, single-celled living things. They are prokaryotes: cells with no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles. Your cells are eukaryotes: they have a nucleus and organelles like mitochondria.

Parts of a bacterium:
Pilitiny hairs for gripping surfaces Cell walltough outer layer for protection Cell membranecontrols what enters and exits DNAfloats free — no nucleus! Ribosomesbuild proteins Flagellumtail for swimming

Real size: about 1–2 micrometers. 500 bacteria could line up across the head of a pin.

  • Reproduce fast: some bacteria split in two (binary fission) every 20 minutes. One cell can become millions overnight.
  • Helpful: digest your food, make vitamins, make yogurt and cheese.
  • Harmful: a few cause diseases like strep throat, cholera, and the plague. These are called pathogens.
  • Neutral: most just live their lives and don't affect us at all.
Connect: Ribosomes still build proteins and the cell membrane still controls what goes in and out, just like in the cells from our last unit.
2

Plague! How Disease Spreads

From 1347 to 1351, the Black Death swept across Europe, Asia, and North Africa. It killed an estimated 25–50 million people in Europe — roughly 1 out of every 3 people. It was caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis, carried by fleas that lived on rats.

Infectious diseases spread from person to person (or animal to person). Three things make them spread faster:

  1. Crowding (population density): more people packed together means more contact.
  2. Mixing: people traveling and meeting new groups (trade ships carried plague fleas port to port).
  3. Poor hygiene: dirty water, no hand washing, and trash attract germs and rats.

But not everyone who caught it died. People have variations in their immune systems. Scientists studying old skeletons found that people with certain immune system genes were more likely to survive — and they passed those genes to their kids.

3

Survival of the Fittest: Natural Selection

Natural selection is how a population changes over many generations. Living things with traits that help them survive live longer, have more babies, and pass those traits on. Remember it with VISTA:

LetterStands forIn the mouse example
VVariationMice come in light and dark fur
IInheritanceFur color is passed from parents to kids
SSelectionLight mice get eaten more on dark lava rock
TTimeOver many generations, more dark mice survive
AAdaptationDark fur is now an adaptation in this habitat
Important! Individuals do not change on purpose. The population changes because survivors pass on their traits. New variations come from random mutations (small changes in DNA).
4

Superbugs: Antibiotic Resistance

In 1928, Alexander Fleming noticed mold killing bacteria in a dish. That mold made penicillin, the first antibiotic. Antibiotics have saved hundreds of millions of lives. But bacteria are evolving. Antibiotic resistance is natural selection happening fast:

  1. In a group of bacteria, a few randomly have a mutation that helps them survive the medicine.
  2. The antibiotic kills the weak bacteria. The resistant ones survive.
  3. The survivors reproduce every 20 minutes and pass on resistance.
  4. Soon the whole population is resistant: a superbug.
Antibiotics do NOT work on viruses (like colds or flu). Taking them when you don't need them just gives bacteria more practice at becoming resistant.

How antibiotics attack cell parts you know: 🧱 Cell wall breakers (penicillin) → water rushes in by osmosis and the bacterium bursts. ⚙️ Ribosome blockers → the cell can't make proteins. 🚪 Superbug trick: pump proteins use active transport to shove medicine back out!

New weapon: bacteriophages

Bacteriophages ("phages") are viruses that only attack bacteria. In 2016, doctors saved a man dying from a superbug infection by giving him phages when no antibiotic worked. This is called phage therapy.

5

Your Body's Home Team: The Microbiome

Your microbiome is all the tiny living things that live in and on you, mostly in your gut. You carry about 38 trillion bacteria — about the same as the number of human cells in your body!

Think of your gut like a soccer team:

  • Defense: good bacteria take up all the space and food, so harmful bacteria can't move in.
  • Midfield: they break down fiber and make vitamins like vitamin K.
  • Coaching: they help train your immune system.
Connect: Living things that depend on each other in a way that helps both is called mutualism. You give gut bacteria a warm home and food; they digest food and protect you.
6

Team Bacteria: Talking and Working Together

Many bacteria build a biofilm: a slimy "fort" of bacteria glued together. The plaque on your teeth in the morning is a biofilm! Biofilms can make bacteria up to 1,000 times harder to kill with antibiotics.

How do they coordinate? They "talk" with chemical signals — this is called quorum sensing. Each bacterium releases a little signal. When enough bacteria are present, the signal gets strong and they all act at once.

New idea: Scientists like Dr. Bonnie Bassler are designing medicines that block the signals, so bacteria never "hear" each other and never launch their attack.
7

Final Mission: Stop the Outbreak

Cholera is caused by Vibrio cholerae. It spreads through water or food contaminated with sewage. In 1854 London, doctor John Snow mapped cholera deaths and traced them to one water pump. When the pump handle was removed, cases dropped — one of the first uses of data to stop an outbreak.

For your final mission, choose the best strategy and defend it with a claim, evidence, and reasoning:

  • 🚰 Clean water, sanitation & hygiene
  • 💊 Smarter antibiotic use
  • 🦠 Phage therapy
  • 🏡 Protect the microbiome
  • 📡 Block bacteria's signals

Why is this a big deal?

For about 100 years, deaths from infectious diseases went down thanks to clean water, vaccines, and antibiotics. Now superbugs, crowded cities, and fast travel are pushing some of those diseases back up. That's the puzzle this whole unit is about.

For our athletes ⚽🏐

Superbugs in the Locker Room

MRSA and athletes

MRSA is a staph bacterium resistant to many common antibiotics. The CDC says it can spread among athletes through close contact, shared towels and gear, and turf burns or scrapes.

CDC game plan for athletes

  • Wash hands before and after practice and games.
  • Shower right after exercise. Don't share towels, bar soap, or razors.
  • Wash and fully dry your uniform after every use.
  • Cover cuts and turf burns with a clean bandage until healed.
  • Tell a parent or coach right away about red, warm, swollen, or painful skin spots.
