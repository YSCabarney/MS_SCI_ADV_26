Humans vs. Bacteria

Bacteria are everywhere: in the soil, in the ocean, on your soccer ball, and inside you. Most of them are harmless or helpful. A few can make us very sick. And some are learning to beat our best medicines.

Before we start

You already know more than you think

Remember our cells units? A bacterium is one single cell, so everything you learned about cell parts and cell transport is about to come back. Here's how:

From: Cell Theory

All living things are made of cells

A bacterium is a whole living thing made of just one cell. It eats, grows, and reproduces all by itself.

From: Organelles

Bacteria have no nucleus

Their DNA floats in the cytoplasm. They still have a cell membrane, ribosomes, and a tough cell wall, like the plant cells we studied.

From: Cell Transport

Osmosis can pop a bacterium

Penicillin breaks the cell wall. Water rushes in by osmosis and the cell bursts. Some superbugs use active transport pumps to push medicine back out!

Fun fact

Your mitochondria used to be bacteria

Scientists think that over a billion years ago, a bacterium moved into a bigger cell and never left. Today we call it the mitochondrion.

Big Ideas

1

Meet the Microbes

What are bacteria, and how are they different from our cells?
A bacterium (prokaryote). Real size: about 1–2 micrometers, so 500 could line up across the head of a pin.

Bacteria are tiny, single-celled living things. They are prokaryotes: cells with no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles. Your cells are eukaryotes: they have a nucleus and organelles like mitochondria.

  • Reproduce fast: some bacteria split in two (binary fission) every 20 minutes. One cell can become millions overnight.
  • Helpful: digest your food, make vitamins, make yogurt and cheese.
  • Harmful: a few cause diseases like strep throat, cholera, and the plague. These are called pathogens.
  • Neutral: most just live their lives and don't affect us at all.
Connect: Ribosomes still build proteins and the cell membrane still controls what goes in and out, just like in the cells from our last unit.
2

Plague! How Disease Spreads

What can the Black Death teach us? Why did some people survive?

From 1347 to 1351, the Black Death swept across Europe, Asia, and North Africa. It killed an estimated 25–50 million people in Europe, roughly 1 out of every 3 people. It was caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis, carried by fleas that lived on rats.

Infectious diseases spread from person to person (or animal to person). Three things make them spread faster:

  1. Crowding (population density): more people packed together means more contact.
  2. Mixing: people traveling and meeting new groups (trade ships carried plague fleas port to port).
  3. Poor hygiene: dirty water, no hand washing, and trash attract germs and rats.

But not everyone who caught it died. People have variations (differences) in their immune systems. Scientists studying old skeletons found that people with certain immune system genes were more likely to survive, and they passed those genes to their kids.

3

Survival of the Fittest: Natural Selection

How do populations change over time?

Natural selection is how a population changes over many generations. Living things with traits that help them survive in their environment live longer, have more babies, and pass those traits on. Remember it with VISTA:

Real example: rock pocket mice living on dark lava rock in the Arizona and New Mexico desert (studied by HHMI scientists).

Important!

Individuals do not change on purpose. A light mouse can't decide to turn dark. The population changes because the survivors pass on their traits. New variations come from random mutations (small changes in DNA).

4

Superbugs: Antibiotic Resistance

Why aren't antibiotics working as well as they used to?

In 1928, Alexander Fleming noticed mold killing bacteria in a dish. That mold made penicillin, the first antibiotic (a medicine that kills bacteria or stops them from growing). Antibiotics have saved hundreds of millions of lives.

But bacteria are evolving. Antibiotic resistance is natural selection happening fast:

  1. In a group of bacteria, a few randomly have a mutation that helps them survive the medicine.
  2. The antibiotic kills the weak bacteria. The resistant ones survive.
  3. The survivors reproduce every 20 minutes or so and pass on resistance.
  4. Soon the whole population is resistant: a superbug. Antibiotics don't work on it anymore.

Antibiotics do NOT work on viruses (like colds or flu). Taking them when you don't need them just gives bacteria more practice at becoming resistant.

New weapon: bacteria-eating viruses

Bacteriophages ("phages") are viruses that only attack bacteria. They look like tiny moon landers. They inject their DNA into a bacterium, make copies of themselves, and burst it open.

In 2016, doctors in San Diego saved a man dying from a superbug infection by giving him phages when no antibiotic worked. This is called phage therapy.

Why finish your prescription? Stopping early can leave the toughest bacteria alive to multiply. Always take medicine exactly how your doctor says.

5

Your Body's Home Team: The Microbiome

How do helpful bacteria keep us healthy?

Your microbiome is all the tiny living things that live in and on you, mostly in your gut (intestines). You carry about 38 trillion bacteria, about the same as the number of human cells in your body!

Think of your gut like a volleyball team. Every position has a job:

  • Defense: good bacteria take up all the space and food, so harmful bacteria can't move in (competition).
  • Midfield: they break down fiber you can't digest and make vitamins like vitamin K.
  • Coaching: they help train your immune system.

Your gut is an ecosystem. Just like a rainforest, an ecosystem with more biodiversity (more kinds of living things) is more stable. Studies show people like the Hadza of Tanzania, who hunt and gather wild foods, have more kinds of gut bacteria than most people in the U.S.

Connect: Living things that depend on each other in a way that helps both is called mutualism. You give gut bacteria a warm home and food; they digest food and protect you.
6

Team Bacteria: Talking and Working Together

How do bacteria cooperate, and can we use that against them?

Bacteria may be single cells, but they act like a team. Many build a biofilm: a slimy "fort" of bacteria glued together. The plaque on your teeth in the morning is a biofilm! Biofilms protect bacteria and can make them up to 1,000 times harder to kill with antibiotics.

How do they coordinate? They "talk" with chemical signals. This is called quorum sensing. Each bacterium releases a little signal chemical. When enough bacteria are around (a quorum, like enough players to start a game), the signal gets strong and they all act at once.

  • Harmful bacteria wait until they have enough numbers, then attack together.
  • New idea: scientists like Dr. Bonnie Bassler are designing medicines that block the signals, so bacteria never "hear" each other and never launch their attack.

Cooperation shows up in many animals, too: humpback whales team up to trap fish in "bubble nets." Group behavior helps both individuals and the whole species survive.

7

Final Mission: Stop the Outbreak

What is the best way to prevent a future cholera outbreak?

Cholera is a disease caused by the bacterium Vibrio cholerae. It spreads through water or food contaminated with sewage. It causes severe diarrhea and can kill in hours from dehydration. It's also easy to treat with oral rehydration solution (clean water + salt + sugar).

In 1854 London, doctor John Snow mapped cholera deaths and traced them to one water pump on Broad Street. When the pump handle was removed, cases dropped. That was one of the first uses of data to stop an outbreak.

Why is this a big deal?

For about 100 years, deaths from infectious diseases went down thanks to clean water, vaccines, and antibiotics. Now superbugs, crowded cities, and fast travel are pushing some of those diseases back up. That's the puzzle this whole unit is about.

For our athletes 🏐

Superbugs in the Locker Room

MRSA and athletes

MRSA is a staph bacterium that is resistant to many common antibiotics. The CDC says it can spread among athletes through close contact, shared towels and gear, and turf burns or scrapes. Volleyball floor dives, digs, and turf burns can all cause the kind of skin scrapes MRSA likes.

CDC game plan for athletes

  • Wash hands before and after practice and games.
  • Shower right after exercise. Don't share towels, bar soap, or razors.
  • Wash and fully dry your uniform after every use.
  • Cover cuts and turf burns with a clean bandage until healed.
  • Tell a parent or coach right away about red, warm, swollen, or painful skin spots.

Natural Selection

Antibiotic Resistance

When bacteria evolve so an antibiotic can no longer kill them.

Bacteriophage

Microbiome

All the microorganisms (mostly bacteria) living in and on your body.

Biodiversity

The number of different kinds of living things in an ecosystem. More diversity = more stable.

New · Stop 5

Mutualism

You and your gut bacteria
New · Stop 6

Biofilm

A slimy layer of bacteria stuck together on a surface that protects them.

New · Stop 6

Quorum Sensing

How bacteria "talk": they release chemical signals and act together once there are enough of them.
Like waiting for a full team to start a game
Review · Cells

Cell Wall

A stiff outer layer outside the cell membrane that gives support. Plants and bacteria have one; animals don't.
Penicillin targets it
Review · Cells

Ribosome

The organelle that builds proteins. Bacterial ribosomes are a bit different from ours.
Some antibiotics jam bacterial ribosomes
Review · Transport

Osmosis

Diffusion of water across a membrane from high water concentration to low.
Without its wall, a bacterium swells and bursts
Review · Transport

Active Transport

Moving materials across a membrane using energy, often against the gradient.
Superbugs pump antibiotics out this way
Review · Transport

Endocytosis

A cell wraps its membrane around something to bring it inside.
