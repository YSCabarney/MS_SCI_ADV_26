Materials

What You'll Need

🧵Foam pipe insulation tubes (pre-slit, ~3.3 ft each)
⚪Steel balls (1 in.), marbles, or other small balls
🔵Painter's tape (blue)
📏Tape measure (25 ft)
✏️Markers & index cards
✂️Scissors (adult helps cut)
⏱️Stopwatch (phone/tablet)
📚Books, boxes, or chairs for supports (ground build)
🧱A clear wall you're allowed to tape (wall build)
🥤A small cup or bowl to catch the ball
🧵String (to measure curved paths)

Track A needs about 3 tubes. Track B needs about 4. Your teacher will tell you how many your team has.

⚠️ Safety Rules — Everyone Follows These

  • Ask an adult to help you cut tubes. Cut away from your body and fingers. Never use a knife.
  • Steel balls are heavy — never throw or drop them. Roll them only on your track, into a catch cup.
  • Small parts are a choking hazard — keep balls away from little kids and pets.
  • Ask permission before taping to walls or furniture, and test a small spot first.
  • Stay low. Don't stand on chairs or tables. Keep release height 60 cm (2 ft) or less.
  • Keep walkways clear so nobody trips on your track.

Your class hasn't formally studied forces yet, so before you start, research and write one paragraph (4–6 sentences) that covers:

Step 1

Testable Question

"How does the height a ball is released from affect its speed at the bottom of the drop, and how fast does the ball need to be going to make it around a loop?"

You change the release height (independent variable) and measure the speed and whether the ball completes the loop (dependent variables). Along the way you'll measure your track's hill and loop to compare speed and velocity at different places.

Part 1

The Science You'll Use

You already learned some of these ideas — here they are again, tied to your coaster.

🧠

In plain English: Distance is the whole path length — like counting every step you actually walked, curves included. Displacement is a straight arrow from where you started to where you ended, even if you wandered a lot to get there.

TermMeaningFormula
DistanceHow far the ball actually travels along its path, curves and all. A scalar (size only, never negative).—
DisplacementStraight-line change in position from start to finish, with direction. A vector — can be small or zero even if the ball traveled far.—
SpeedHow fast the ball is moving. A scalar.distance ÷ time
VelocityHow fast and in which direction. A vector.displacement ÷ time
AccelerationAny change in velocity — speeding up, slowing down, or changing direction. A ball circling a loop is accelerating the whole time, even at constant speed.(final velocity − start velocity) ÷ time
Why the loop is the perfect example: big distance, tiny displacement.
Part 2

Builder's Toolkit

How to work with the foam tubes — read this before you build.

Skill 1 · Make half-pipe tracks

  1. Ask an adult to help. Lay the tube on a table, slit facing up.
  2. Cut along the tube on the side opposite the slit (the bottom), the whole length.
  3. You now have two U-shaped half-pipes from each tube. Check the inside is smooth, with no torn foam the ball could catch on.

Skill 2 · Join two pieces smoothly

  1. Overlap the upstream piece's end over the top of the downstream piece by about 2 cm — the ball should roll down a tiny step, never up a lip.
  2. Tape across the joint on both sides with painter's tape.
  3. Test it by slowly rolling the ball over the joint. If it bumps or stops, adjust the overlap.

Skill 3 · Hold the track up and measure height

  1. Use books, boxes, or chair backs as supports. Tape the tube to the support every 30 cm or so — don't squeeze it flat.
  2. Keep the U-shape upright and level side to side, or the ball will fall out.
  3. If the ball jumps out on a curve, tape a second half-pipe upside down over it like a tunnel roof.
  4. Label your release heights (20, 30, 40, 50 cm) on a strip of tape or an index card.
💡

Pro tip: Release height is measured straight up from the flat bottom of the track to the bottom of the ball, not along the slope. Hold your tape measure vertical.

Skill 4 · Build a hill New

  1. Take one tube piece and place a support (a thin book, about 5–8 cm tall) under its middle, so the piece arches gently up in the center and back down on both ends.
  2. Tape the joints on both sides smoothly into the pieces before and after it — no lips the ball can catch on.
  3. Test it slowly first: does the ball make it over the top, or does it need more speed (a higher release point)?
Side view: a single support under the middle of one piece makes a gentle hill.

Skill 5 · Build a loop

  1. Curve one half-pipe into a circle about as wide as a dinner plate (20–25 cm across) — the ball rolls on the inside.
  2. Make the exit sit beside the entrance, like a corkscrew, so the track doesn't run into itself.
  3. Stand the loop up and tape it to stiff cardboard, a box, or a wall so it stays upright. Add tape "ribs" across the outside to keep it round.
  4. Check the inside is smooth, with tape pressed flat and not blocking the ball.
  5. Mark tape at the loop's entrance and exit at the bottom — you'll use them for timing later.
Troubleshooting
ProblemTry this
Ball jumps out of the trackSmaller/lighter ball, add a tape guard rail, or lower the release height.
Ball stops mid-trackCheck for lips, tape blocking the path, or a sagging tube.
Ball falls off the loopRaise the release height, make the loop smaller/rounder, or smooth the entrance.
Ball can't make it over the hillLower the hill, or raise the release height so it has more speed going in.
Tube keeps floppingAdd more supports, or tape it to cardboard or the wall.
Part 3

Design & Build Your Coaster

Step 1 · Choose your track

Your whole team builds the same track type, so you can compare data. Every track — A or B — needs a hill and a loop.

Standard

Track A · Hill + One Loop

Drop → hill → speed trap 1 → one loop → final speed trap → catch cup. About 3 tubes (5–6 half-pipes).

Challenge

Track B · Hill + Two Loops

Drop → hill → speed trap 1 → loop 1 → middle speed trap → loop 2 → final speed trap → catch cup. About 4 tubes (8–9 half-pipes). Loop 2 is a little smaller than loop 1.

Track A (side view). Distances are a guide — if your space is small, keep speed traps at least 60 cm long.

Step 2 · Choose where to build

Pick whichever fits your space better — the physics and measurements work the same either way.

Option A

Build on the ground

Lay your track across a clear floor space at least 3 m (10 ft) long, held up by books, boxes, or chair backs. Best if you have open floor space.

Option B New

Tape it to a wall

Tape your whole track — hill, loop, and all — up a clear wall you have permission to use, so it descends from high on the wall down to a catch cup on the floor. Best for small spaces.

Same track, two ways to hold it up.
💡

Building on a wall? Release height is measured the exact same way — straight up from the flat bottom of the track at the release mark, using your tape measure held vertical, not along the wall's slope. Use plenty of crossed tape to hold each piece firmly, and keep the catch cup right at the base of the wall where the track ends.

Step 3 · Brainstorm and sketch with your team (10 min on your call)

  • Where's a safe, clear space — floor or wall — at least 3 m (10 ft) of track length?
  • What will hold up the release point, and how will you raise/lower it without rebuilding? (Idea: a stack of books you add or remove.)
  • Where will your hill and loop go? Where will your catch cup sit?

Draw a labeled sketch in your Lab Report's Design Sketch box — label the release mark, hill, speed traps, loop(s), and catch cup.

Step 4 · Build version 1

Meet these requirements so your data will be good:

  • Adjustable height: release point can be set to 20, 30, 40, and 50 cm above the flat bottom.
  • A hill: a gentle rise and fall between the drop and Speed Trap 1.
  • Speed trap 1: a straight, level section right after the hill, at least 60 cm (80 cm is best), with tape marks at each end.
  • Loop(s): upright, 20–25 cm across (Track B: second loop 15–20 cm), with entrance/exit tape marks at the bottom.
  • Final speed trap: straight, level, at least 60 cm, tape marks at each end (Track B also needs a middle speed trap).
  • Catch cup at the end.
  • The ball completes the loop when released from 50 cm.
💡

Your first version probably won't work perfectly — that's normal. Engineers test, find the problem, and rebuild. Write down each change in the Rebuild Log in your Lab Report.

Step 5 · Choose your ball, then keep it the same

Test a steel ball, marbles, or other balls. Pick the one that stays in your track best, and use that same ball for every experiment.

End of Session 1 checkpoint: your ball can roll from the release mark to the catch cup, even if it doesn't make the loop yet. Take a photo of your track and paste it into your Lab Report, along with your sketch.

Part 4

Measure Your Track

Your Variables

Independent variable: release height (cm).
Dependent variables: speed in the speed trap (m/s), and whether the ball completes the loop (yes/no).
Controlled variables: the same ball, the same track shape and layout, the same release method, the same speed trap length, the same person timing, and the same surface the track sits on (or wall it's taped to).

💡 Not sure why these count as "controlled"?
A controlled variable is anything you deliberately keep identical across every trial so that release height is the only thing that's different. If you switched balls halfway through, you wouldn't know if a speed change was from the height or the new ball.

Step 1 · Mark and measure your track (do this once)

  1. Mark the two ends of each speed trap with painter's tape, labeled ST1, ST-Mid (Track B only), and ST-Final.
  2. Measure the length of each speed trap in cm, then convert to meters (divide by 100).
  3. Measure the diameter of each loop (cm).
  4. Distance around a loop: lay string along the track from entrance mark to exit mark, then measure the string. (Or estimate 3.14 × diameter.)
  5. Displacement of the loop: measure the straight-line distance between entrance and exit marks.

Step 2 · How to time the ball

  1. Start the timer the instant the ball touches tape mark 1. Stop it the instant it touches tape mark 2.
  2. Open a stopwatch and practice pressing Start/Stop a few times first.
  3. Release the ball the same way every time — set it on the release mark and let go, don't push or throw it.
  4. Do 3 trials each time and record all three. If a trial goes wrong, redo it and note why.
🧠

In plain English: speed = trap length (m) ÷ average time (s). If it takes your ball 0.5 seconds to cross an 0.8-meter trap, it's going 0.8 ÷ 0.5 = 1.6 meters per second.

Step 3 · Set up your team spreadsheet

Make a tab per experiment in your team's Google Sheet. Type numbers only in data cells (no units) — put units in the headings.

Experiment 1 · Release height vs. speed (everyone does this)

  1. Set release to 20 cm. Release 3 times, timing through ST1. Note if the loop was completed each time.
  2. Repeat for 30, 40, and 50 cm.
  3. Type all times into your Exp 1 tab — the sheet calculates average speed.
  4. As height goes up, what happens to speed? At what height does the ball start making the loop?

Choose your Test Height: after Experiment 1, pick one release height where every teammate's ball completes the loop at least 3 out of 3 times. Experiments 2 and 3 use it.

Experiment 2 · Speed at start vs. end (Investigator A)

  1. At your Test Height, do 3 trials each timing ST1 and ST-Final (Track B: also ST-Mid).
  2. Calculate change in speed = final speed − start speed. A negative number means the ball slowed down.
  3. Percent change = (change in speed ÷ start speed) × 100.
  4. Think: why might the ball be slower at the end? Where did the speed go? (Your Background Research paragraph has a clue.)
💡 Percent change — worked example
Start speed 2.0 m/s, final speed 1.6 m/s. Change = 1.6 − 2.0 = −0.4 m/s. Percent change = (−0.4 ÷ 2.0) × 100 = −20%. The ball lost about 20% of its starting speed.

Experiment 3 · Distance vs. displacement in the loop (Investigator B)

  1. At your Test Height, do 3 trials timing loop entrance mark to exit mark.
  2. Enter the distance and displacement you measured in Step 1.
  3. The sheet calculates average speed (distance ÷ time) and average velocity (displacement ÷ time). Compare them. (Track B: repeat for Loop 2.)

Experiment 4 · Minimum height & speed to make the loop (Investigator C)

  1. Start at a height where the ball fails the loop (e.g. 20 cm). Raise it 5 cm at a time.
  2. At each height, do 3 runs and record Yes/No. Stop when it completes the loop 3 out of 3 — that's your minimum loop height (Track B: both loops).
  3. At that height, do 3 timed runs through ST1 and calculate speed — that's about the minimum speed needed to make the loop.

Investigators A, B, and C: paste your results into the team sheet so everyone has all four experiments for the Lab Report.

Part 5

Graph & Analyze

Graph 1 · Release height vs. speed (scatter chart with trendline)

  1. In the Exp 1 tab, select the Release height and Average speed columns.
  2. Insert → Chart → Scatter chart. Make sure the X-axis is Release height.
  3. Add a title and axis titles (Release height (cm) / Average speed (m/s)). Turn on the Trendline.
  4. Paste the chart into your Lab Report (unlinked) with a caption.

Team challenge: each teammate's data as a different color series on the same graph — how similar are your results?

Analyze

  • Describe the pattern: does the trendline keep rising, or level off?
  • What release height and speed did it take to complete the loop?
  • Compare average speed with average velocity for the loop.
  • What happened to speed from start to end of the track — and what force was responsible? (Tie this back to your Background Research.)
  • As the ball climbed your hill, which force slowed it down? As it came back down, which force sped it back up?
  • Reliability: how close are your three trials? Your teammates' results?

Finish the Lab

Conclusion (Claim – Evidence – Reasoning): Claim — answer the testable question in 1–2 sentences. Evidence — use at least three numbers from your tables or graph. Reasoning — explain why your evidence supports your claim, using the words speed, velocity, distance, displacement, and acceleration.

Discussion questions: the Lab Report ends with 10 short-essay questions, 3–5 sentences each with specific evidence from your lab. Talk them over as a team, then write your own answers in your own words.

Glossary

ForceA push or a pull on an object.
GravityThe force pulling your ball down toward the ground the whole time it's on the track.
FrictionA force that resists motion when things rub or roll against each other.
ScalarA quantity with size only, like distance and speed.
VectorA quantity with size and direction, like displacement and velocity.
Speed trapA marked section of track used to measure speed.
TrialOne run of the experiment. More trials make your data more trustworthy.
TrendlineA line on a graph showing the general pattern of the points.
Independent / dependent / controlled variableWhat you change / what you measure / what you keep the same.
