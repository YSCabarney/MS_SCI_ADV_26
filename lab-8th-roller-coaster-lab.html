8th Grade Physical Science · Team Lab (groups of 3)

The Coaster Lab

Design, build, test, and rebuild a marble roller coaster out of foam pipe insulation. Then use your measurements to describe its motion with speed, velocity, distance, and displacement.

Time: 2–3 class periods (about 30 min each) Tracks: Track A (one loop) or Track B (two loops)
Our Testable Question

How does the height a ball is released from affect its speed at the bottom of the drop, and how fast does the ball need to be going to make it around a loop?

You change the release height (independent variable) and measure the speed and whether the ball completes the loop (dependent variables). Along the way you will measure different parts of your track to compare speed and velocity at different places.

Materials

From your teacher

  • Foam pipe insulation tubes (pre-slit, about 3.3 feet each)
  • Steel balls (1 inch) — and/or marbles or other small balls
  • Painter's tape (blue)
  • Tape measure (25 ft)
  • Markers
  • Index cards

From home (ask an adult first)

  • Scissors
  • Stopwatch (phone, tablet, or Chromebook)
  • Stacks of books, boxes, chairs, or a cardboard sheet for supports
  • A small cup or bowl to catch the ball
  • A clear, safe floor space about 3 m (10 ft) long
  • String (to measure curved paths)

Tubes you will need: Track A uses about 3 tubes. Track B uses about 4 tubes. Your teacher will tell you how many your team has.

Safety rules — everyone follows these
  • Cutting: ask an adult to help you cut the tubes. Use scissors, cut away from your body and fingers, and never use a knife unless an adult does the cutting.
  • Steel balls are heavy. Never throw or drop them. Roll them only on your track. Use a catch cup so they do not roll away or hit walls, pets, or people.
  • Small parts. A 1-inch ball can be a choking hazard. Keep it away from little kids and pets.
  • Painter's tape is made to peel off, but ask permission before taping to walls, furniture, or painted surfaces. Test a small spot first.
  • Stay low. Do not stand on chairs or tables. Keep your release height 60 cm (2 feet) or less.
  • Keep walkways clear so nobody trips on your track.
PART 1

The science you will use

≈ 5 min read

You already learned these ideas. Here they are again, tied to your coaster.

Distance

How far the ball actually travels along its path. Includes every curve and loop. It is a scalar: size only, no direction. It is never negative.

Displacement

The straight-line change in position from start to finish, with direction. It is a vector. It can be small or even zero, even if the ball traveled far.

Speed

How fast the ball is moving. Speed is a scalar.

speed = distance ÷ time

Velocity

How fast and in which direction. Velocity is a vector.

velocity = displacement ÷ time
Acceleration (needed for some discussion questions)
acceleration = (final velocity − starting velocity) ÷ time

Acceleration means any change in velocity: speeding up, slowing down, or changing direction. A ball going around a loop is changing direction the whole time, so it is accelerating even if its speed stays the same.

PART 2

Builder's toolkit: how to work with the foam tubes

≈ 5 min read

Skill 1 · Make half-pipe tracks

Your foam tube has a slit down one side. To make a track, you need an open "U" shape (a half-pipe) so the ball can roll on it.

  1. Ask an adult to help. Lay the tube on a table with the slit facing up.

  2. Use scissors to cut along the tube on the side opposite the slit (the bottom), the whole length. Cut slowly, and keep your other hand away from the blades.

  3. Now you have two U-shaped half-pipes from each tube. Check the inside: it should be smooth with no bumps or torn foam that the ball could catch on.

Skill 2 · Join two pieces smoothly

  1. Put the upstream piece (the one the ball comes from first) so its end overlaps the top of the downstream piece by about 2 cm. The ball should roll down a tiny step, never up a lip.

  2. Tape across the joint on the outside of the tubes with painter's tape. Add a second piece of tape on the other side.

  3. Test it: slowly roll the ball over the joint. If it bumps, wiggles, or stops, adjust the overlap.

Skill 3 · Hold the track up and measure its height

  • Use books, boxes, or chair backs as supports. Tape the tube to the support every 30 cm or so with painter's tape. Do not squeeze the tube flat.
  • Keep the U-shape upright and level from side to side. If the track tilts sideways, the ball will fall out.
  • Add tape "guard rails" if the ball jumps out on a curve: tape a second half-pipe upside down over the curve like a tunnel roof.
  • Use a marker to draw a release mark and label your release heights (20, 30, 40, 50 cm) on a strip of painter's tape or an index card.

Skill 4 · Build a loop

  1. Curve one half-pipe into a circle about as wide as a dinner plate (20–25 cm across). The ball will roll on the inside of the circle.

  2. Make the exit sit beside the entrance, like a corkscrew, so the track does not run into itself. The tube leaves the loop about one tube-width to the side of where it came in.

  3. Stand the loop up. Tape it to a stiff piece of cardboard, a box, or a wall so it stays upright. Add a few pieces of tape across the outside of the loop like ribs to keep it round.

  4. Check the inside of the loop: tape must be pressed flat and not block the ball.

  5. Mark tape marks at the loop's entrance and exit at the bottom (you will use them for timing in Experiment 3).

Troubleshooting your build
  • Ball jumps out of the track: smaller or lighter ball, add a tape guard rail, or lower the release height.
  • Ball stops in the middle: check for lips, tape blocking the track, or a sagging tube.
  • Ball falls off the loop: raise the release height, make the loop smaller or rounder, or make the entrance smoother.
  • Tube keeps flopping: add more supports or tape it to cardboard.
  • A 1-inch steel ball is too wide for your track: try a marble or smaller ball. Use the same ball for the whole lab.

PART 4

Measure your track

Sessions 2 and 3
Your variables
  • Independent variable: release height (cm).
  • Dependent variables: speed in the speed trap (m/s), and whether the ball completes the loop (yes/no).
  • Controlled variables (keep these the same): the same ball, the same track shape and layout, the same release method, the same speed trap length, the same person timing, and the same surface the track sits on.

Step 1 · Mark and measure your track (do this once)

  1. Use painter's tape to mark the two ends of each speed trap. Use a marker to label them ST1, ST-Mid (Track B only), and ST-Final.

  2. Measure the length of each speed trap with the tape measure (in cm). Write it in your Lab Report. Then change it into meters (divide by 100).

  3. Measure the diameter of each loop (in cm).

  4. Distance around a loop: lay a piece of string along the track from the loop's entrance mark to its exit mark. Mark the string, then measure the string with your tape measure. (Or estimate 3.14 × diameter.)

  5. Displacement of the loop: measure the straight-line distance between the entrance mark and the exit mark.

Step 2 · How to time the ball

  1. Open a stopwatch on your phone, tablet, or Chromebook. Practice pressing Start and Stop several times.

  2. Release the ball the same way every time: set it on the release mark and just let go. Do not push or throw it. Use one hand to release and the other to run the stopwatch, or ask a family member to help.

  3. Do 3 trials each time and record all three times. If a trial goes wrong (the ball wobbles out, or you press late), do it again and write a note.

  4. Speed = trap length (m) ÷ average time (s).

Optional accuracy boost: slow-motion video
  1. Set a phone or tablet on a stack of books so it looks straight at the speed trap. Use the slow-motion video setting if you have one.
  2. Record the ball rolling through the trap.
  3. Play the video back slowly. Start and stop your stopwatch at the tape marks. If you played it at 1/4 speed, multiply your stopwatch time by 0.25 to get the real time.

Step 3 · Set up your team spreadsheet

Open your team's Google Sheet and make a tab for each experiment. Need help? Use the Google Sheets Graphing Guide. Type the headings in Row 1.

TabHeadings and formulas (row 2 shown)

Exp 3A: Loop (1 or 2) · B: Distance (m) · C: Displacement (m) · D, E, F: Times 1–3 (s) · G: Average time (s) =AVERAGE(D2:F2) · H: Average speed (m/s) =B2/G2 · I: Average velocity (m/s) =C2/G2
Exp 4A: Release height tried (cm) · B, C, D: Completed loop? (Yes/No) · E: Times completed (out of 3)

Type numbers only in data cells (no units). Put the units in the headings. Convert cm to m by dividing by 100 before you type a length into a "(m)" column.

Experiment 1 · Release height vs. speed at the bottom (everyone does this)

Goal: find out how release height changes the speed in Speed Trap 1, and record whether the ball makes the loop.

  1. Set the release point to 20 cm. Release the ball 3 times. Time each run through ST1. After each run, note if the ball completed the loop (Yes/No). If it did not, still record the ST1 time.

  2. Repeat for 30 cm, 40 cm, and 50 cm. (If your track cannot handle these heights, use heights that are 10 cm apart, like 15, 25, 35, 45, and tell your team.)

  3. Type all times into your Exp 1 tab. The sheet calculates the average speed. Count how many of the 3 runs completed the loop.

  4. Look at your numbers. As height goes up, what happens to speed? At what height does the ball start to make the loop?

Example row (practice numbers, not real results)

Height 30 cm · times 0.52 s, 0.49 s, 0.55 s · average 0.52 s · trap length 0.80 m · speed = 0.80 ÷ 0.52 = 1.5 m/s.

Choose your Test Height: after Experiment 1, your team picks one release height where every teammate's ball completes the loop at least 3 out of 3 times. Call it the Test Height. Experiments 2 and 3 use it.

Experiment 2 · Speed at the start vs. the end (Investigator A)

Goal: compare how fast the ball moves right after the drop and near the end of the track.

  1. Set the release point to your Test Height.

  2. Do 3 trials timing ST1. (You can reuse Experiment 1 data at that height if it is the same setup.)

  3. Do 3 trials timing ST-Final. (Track B: also do 3 trials for ST-Mid.)

  4. Calculate the average speed for each trap. Then calculate the change in speed = final speed − start speed. A negative number means the ball slowed down.

  5. Find the percent change: (change in speed ÷ start speed) × 100.

Think: why might the ball be slower at the end? Where did the speed go?

Experiment 3 · Distance vs. displacement in the loop (Investigator B)

Goal: use the loop to show how average speed and average velocity can be very different.

  1. Set the release point to your Test Height.

  2. Do 3 trials timing the ball from the loop entrance mark (start timer) to the loop exit mark (stop timer).

  3. Type in the distance (path around the loop) and the displacement (straight line from entrance mark to exit mark) you measured in Step 1.

  4. The sheet calculates average speed = distance ÷ time and average velocity = displacement ÷ time. Compare them. (Track B: repeat for Loop 2.)

Example (practice numbers)

Distance 0.69 m · displacement 0.04 m · time 0.55 s → average speed = 0.69 ÷ 0.55 = 1.25 m/s; average velocity = 0.04 ÷ 0.55 = 0.07 m/s in the direction of the exit.

Experiment 4 · Minimum height and speed to make the loop (Investigator C)

Goal: find the lowest release height that gets the ball around the loop, and how fast it is going at the bottom when it does.

  1. Start at a height where the ball fails to complete the loop (for example, 20 cm).

  2. Raise the release point 5 cm at a time. At each height, do 3 runs and record Yes or No for each. Stop when the ball completes the loop 3 times out of 3. That height is your minimum loop height. (Track B: the ball must complete both loops.)

  3. At that minimum height, do 3 timed runs through ST1. Calculate the speed. This is (about) the minimum speed needed at the bottom to make the loop.

  4. Record your results in the Exp 4 tab and the Lab Report.

Share your results

Investigators A, B, and C: paste your results into the team sheet and tell your team what you found. Everyone needs the results from all four experiments for the Lab Report.

PART 5

Graph and analyze

Session 3

Graph 1 · Release height vs. speed (scatter chart with trendline)

Open the Graphing Guide (Example 2)

  1. In the Exp 1 tab, highlight the Release height column and the Average speed column. (Hold Ctrl and click-drag to select two columns that are not next to each other.)

  2. Click Insert → Chart. In the Chart editor, choose Scatter chart. Make sure the X-axis is Release height.

  3. Add a chart title, a horizontal axis title (Release height (cm)), and a vertical axis title (Average speed (m/s)). Turn on the Trendline.

  4. Copy the chart into your Lab Report (Paste unlinked). Write a caption.

  5. Team challenge: each teammate's data can be a different color series on the same graph. Are your three results similar? How much do they differ?

Analyze

  • Describe the pattern: what does the trendline show? Does speed keep going up steadily, or does it level off?
  • Loop threshold: what release height and speed did it take to complete the loop? Mark it on your graph.
  • Vectors: compare average speed with average velocity for the loop.
  • Start vs. end: what happened to the speed as the ball traveled?
  • Reliability: how close are your three trials? How close are your teammates' results?
PART 6

Finish the lab

Conclusion (Claim – Evidence – Reasoning)

  • Claim: answer the testable question in one or two sentences.
  • Evidence: use at least three numbers from your tables or graph.
  • Reasoning: explain why the evidence supports your claim. Use the words speed, velocity, distance, displacement, and acceleration.

Discussion questions

The Lab Report ends with 10 short-essay questions. Answer each in 3–5 sentences, with specific evidence from your lab. Talk them over as a team, then write your own answers in your own words.

Clean up

GLOSSARY

Words to know

WordMeaning
ScalarA quantity with size only (like distance and speed).
VectorA quantity with size and direction (like displacement and velocity).
DistanceTotal path length traveled.
DisplacementStraight-line change in position from start to finish, with direction.
SpeedDistance ÷ time.
VelocityDisplacement ÷ time, with direction.
AccelerationChange in velocity ÷ time. Speeding up, slowing down, or turning.
Speed trapA marked section of track used to measure speed.
TrialOne run of the experiment. More trials make your data more trustworthy.
FrictionA force that resists motion when things rub or roll against each other.
Independent / dependent / controlled variableWhat you change / what you measure / what you keep the same.
TrendlineA line on a graph that shows the general pattern of the points.
