Before We Open This File

In your last unit, you learned that scientists test ideas by controlling variables — keeping some things the same while changing one thing on purpose (the independent variable) and measuring what happens as a result (the dependent variable).

Motion is just another thing scientists investigate the exact same way. When we study how something moves, time is almost always the independent variable — it ticks forward no matter what. Position (and later, velocity) is the dependent variable — it changes in response to time passing. Every graph and formula in this file is really just: "as time changes, what happens to position?"

CASE 01

Position-Time Graphs

To describe motion, scientists first need a reference point — a fixed spot to measure everything from. Your position is simply your location compared to that reference point.

Reading the Graph A position-time graph plots time on the x-axis and position on the y-axis. The steepness of the line — its slope — tells you how fast the position is changing. A steeper line means faster motion. A flat line means the object isn't moving at all.
Evidence Check

1. Using the graph above, what is the hiker's position at t = 2 s?

Reveal Answer
6 meters — read straight up from t = 2 on the x-axis to the line, then across to the y-axis.

2. What is the slope of this line, and what does that number actually represent?

Reveal Answer
Slope = rise ÷ run = 12 m ÷ 4 s = 3 m/s. On a position-time graph, the slope is the object's speed — so the hiker is moving at 3 meters every second.

3. A different graph shows a hiker's line going perfectly flat (horizontal) from t = 10 min to t = 15 min. What's happening during that time?

Reveal Answer
The hiker has stopped. Position isn't changing even though time is still passing — a flat line always means zero speed.
CASE 02

Speed vs. Velocity

These two words get used interchangeably in everyday life — but to a scientist, they are not the same thing. This is the heart of today's file.

Speed How fast something is moving. Speed only cares about distance — the total path length traveled — divided by time. It has no direction and is never negative.
speed = distance ÷ time
Velocity How fast, and which way. Velocity uses displacement — the straight-line change from start to finish, including direction — divided by time.
velocity = displacement ÷ time

Speed is a scalar — magnitude (size) only. Velocity is a vector — magnitude and direction. That one difference — direction — changes everything about how the two are calculated.

SimilarBoth measure "how fast." Both are calculated by dividing something by time. Both use the same units (m/s, km/h, mph).

Real-World Case: The 400m Lap

A runner sprints one full lap of a 400 m track in 80 seconds and ends up back exactly where she started. Her distance traveled is 400 m, so her average speed is 400 ÷ 80 = 5 m/s. But her displacement — the straight-line distance between her start and end point — is 0 m, because she finished where she began. That makes her average velocity 0 m/s, even though she was sprinting the entire time. Speed and velocity told two completely different stories about the same run.

Evidence Check

1. True or False: Velocity can be zero even if an object was moving fast the whole time. Explain your answer.

Reveal Answer
True. Velocity depends on displacement (start-to-finish), not on how much ground was actually covered. If an object ends up back at its starting point, average velocity is 0 — regardless of speed.

2. A cyclist bikes 15 km east in 30 minutes, then turns around and bikes 15 km west back to the start in the next 30 minutes. Find: total distance, total displacement, average speed, and average velocity (1 hour total).

Reveal Answer
Distance = 30 km. Displacement = 0 km. Average speed = 30 km ÷ 1 h = 30 km/h. Average velocity = 0 km ÷ 1 h = 0 km/h.

3. A soccer player dribbles 20 m downfield in 5 s (call this the positive direction), then dribbles 20 m back toward her own goal in the next 5 s. What is her velocity during each 5-second stretch?

Reveal Answer
First stretch: +4 m/s (downfield). Second stretch: −4 m/s (the negative sign shows she reversed direction). Same speed both times (4 m/s), but opposite velocities.

4. Reflect: Describe a moment from your own life when your speed and your velocity would have been different from each other.

Reveal Answer
No single right answer — any situation where you returned to a starting point works: pacing during a warm-up, running suicides in practice, walking laps around your kitchen, riding an elevator up and back down.
CASE 03

Units of Measurement

Speed and velocity are measured in a distance unit over a time unit. Which exact units you use depends on who you're talking to.

UnitWhere it's standardWhen to use it
m/s (meters/second)Science & scientific research, worldwideLab reports, formulas, physics class — it's the SI unit, so it plays nicely with every other metric measurement.
km/hMost countries outside the U.S.Everyday speeds — road signs, weather/wind reports, car speedometers.
mphUnited States, everyday useU.S. road signs, car speedometers, weather reports.
Fun Fact / Conversion

1 m/s ≈ 2.24 mph ≈ 3.6 km/h. Usain Bolt's recorded top speed during his 100 m world record run was about 12.4 m/s — roughly 27.8 mph. Scientists reported it in m/s first because that's the international standard for sports science and research.

Evidence Check

1. Which unit would a physicist most likely use in a lab report: m/s, mph, or km/h? Why?

Reveal Answer
m/s — it's the SI (scientific) unit, so it stays consistent with every other measurement (meters, seconds, kilograms) used in a calculation.

2. A U.S. highway sign reads "Speed Limit 45." What unit is implied, and how do you know?

Reveal Answer
Miles per hour (mph) — that's the everyday unit used on U.S. roads and car speedometers.

3. A cheetah sprints at about 30 m/s in a short burst. Using 1 m/s ≈ 2.24 mph, is that faster or slower than a car traveling 60 mph?

Reveal Answer
30 × 2.24 ≈ 67 mph — the cheetah's sprint is faster than a car going 60 mph.
CASE 04

Acceleration

Definition Acceleration is the rate at which velocity changes over time — speeding up, slowing down, or changing direction all count.
acceleration = (final velocity − initial velocity) ÷ time
Measured in meters per second, per second — written m/s².
  • Positive acceleration — speeding up (in the positive direction).
  • Negative acceleration (deceleration) — slowing down.
  • Constant velocity — zero acceleration, even if the object is moving fast, as long as speed and direction never change.
Worked Example — Off the Blocks

A sprinter starts from rest (0 m/s) and reaches 8 m/s just 2 seconds after the starting gun. a = (8 − 0) ÷ 2 = 4 m/s². Now imagine a cyclist braking from 10 m/s down to 2 m/s over 4 seconds: a = (2 − 10) ÷ 4 = −2 m/s² — the negative sign shows she's slowing down.

Evidence Check

1. A soccer player accelerates from 1 m/s to 5 m/s in 2 seconds while sprinting for a loose ball. What is her acceleration?

Reveal Answer
(5 − 1) ÷ 2 = 2 m/s².

2. A skateboarder rolling at 6 m/s comes to a complete stop in 3 seconds. Calculate the acceleration, and explain what the sign means.

Reveal Answer
(0 − 6) ÷ 3 = −2 m/s². The negative sign means she's decelerating (slowing down), not speeding up in a negative direction.

3. Can an object accelerate without getting faster? Give an example.

Reveal Answer
Yes — acceleration means any change in velocity, and velocity includes direction. A runner rounding a curve on a track at a constant speed is still accelerating, because her direction keeps changing.
FIELD REPORTS

Why This Matters — Right Now

Speed, velocity, and acceleration aren't just vocabulary words — they're the exact numbers that athletic trainers, physical therapists, and biomechanists use every day to keep athletes performing and safe.

Wearable GPS Trackers

Many college and professional soccer, basketball, and football teams have athletes wear small GPS trackers between their shoulder blades during practice. The devices record real-time speed, distance covered, and acceleration "loads," so athletic trainers can spot fatigue or overtraining before it turns into an injury.

Sprint-Start Biomechanics

Track coaches film sprinters leaving the starting blocks frame by frame — essentially building a position-time graph out of video — to calculate acceleration and fine-tune technique for a faster start.

Impact Sensors

Some football helmets and mouthguards contain sensors that measure acceleration (g-forces) during hits, giving athletic trainers data to help track and manage head-impact exposure.

Motion-Capture Recovery Tracking

Physical therapists and biomechanists use motion-capture cameras to measure an injured athlete's velocity and acceleration during exercises like a jump or a cutting movement — comparing the numbers over time to decide when it's safe to return to play.

Case Closed — Key Takeaways

  • Position-time graphs show location over time; the slope of the line equals speed.
  • Speed = distance ÷ time (scalar, never negative). Velocity = displacement ÷ time (vector, direction matters).
  • Use m/s in science contexts; mph or km/h in everyday life.
  • Acceleration = change in velocity ÷ time, measured in m/s² — includes speeding up, slowing down, or turning.
  • These exact numbers are how athletic trainers and biomechanists protect and improve real athletes today.
