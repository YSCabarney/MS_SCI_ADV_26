Session 1 · Part A

Salty Spuds — Setting Up the Experiment

🏐 Remember the volleyball gym? The net was like a cell membrane. The volleyballs were molecules. When you spread them evenly on your own (no instructions) — that was diffusion. When a coach told you to push them all to one crowded side — that was active transport (and exhausting!). Today's experiment shows you the exact same thing, just inside a potato cell.

🧪 What we're testing

Testable question: Does the amount of salt in the water change how much a potato strip's length changes after soaking?

Your hypothesis: "If I soak a potato strip in water with more salt, then its length will _______, because water will move _______ the potato cells by osmosis."

Think of it like this: water always moves toward the saltier side, trying to "balance things out." A potato cell is slightly salty inside. If the water outside is way saltier, which direction will water go — IN to the cell, or OUT of the cell?

💡 What to expect from the results

Changes are usually small — 1 to 5 mm. That's real science, not a mistake! The floppy score often shows the effect more clearly. A strip that lost water will feel limp and droopy. A strip that gained water will feel crisp and stiff.

Q 2

Microscope Observations

Use the microscope simulator — click through the time points and try both salt and no-salt. Then answer:

  1. Describe what you see on the salted side at 3 min and at 6 min. What's happening to the cells?
  2. What does the plain side look like throughout? Why do we need it — what's its job in this experiment?
  3. Is water moving into or out of the cells on the salted side? How can you tell?
  4. How is this the exact same process happening inside your potato strips right now?
Watch the size of the vacuole (the big water-filled bubble inside each cell). When a cell loses water, the vacuole shrinks and the cell membrane pulls away from the cell wall — that's called plasmolysis. When a cell gains water, the vacuole swells up. Can you see this happening on the salted side?
📋 Answer Doc → Q2a through Q2d

🌍 Real world connections: This is why cooks salt cucumbers before cooking (pulls water out to keep them crispy). It's why a slug shrivels when salted. It's why you can't drink seawater — it would pull water OUT of your cells instead of hydrating you. It's also why athletes need electrolytes in their sports drinks, not just plain water.

Zoom In — What's Happening at the Cell Membrane

Now we zoom all the way in 🔬

You've seen the potato strips change size. You've seen cucumber cells shrink. But why does this happen? Below, you'll watch the actual molecules moving across the cell membrane — just like they did in your potato experiment. Pick a transport type and watch the animation. Try all four!

Cell Membrane — Live View

Osmosis — Water molecules move across the membrane from the plain-water side (left) toward the salty side (right). The membrane has tiny water-channel proteins called aquaporins. Watch the water level rise on the right as water crosses. Salt is too big to fit through — it stays put. This is exactly what happened in Cup 3. Passive — no energy needed

Q 3

Connect the Lab to the Cell

Watch all 4 animation tabs. Then answer in your doc:

  1. Which transport type caused the potato strips to change size? How do you know — did it use energy?
  2. In Cup 3 (lots of salt), water moved OUT of the potato cells. Was that going from HIGH → LOW or LOW → HIGH concentration? Was energy needed?
  3. Active Transport moves things from LOW → HIGH concentration (uphill). Why does that require energy when osmosis doesn't? Use the words gradient and concentration.
  4. The volleyball game modeled one of these transport types. Which one, and how? (Think about Round 1 vs Round 2.)
Round 1: you spread balls evenly on your own — nobody forced you, they just naturally spread out (like diffusion/osmosis). Round 2: coach said push all balls to the already-crowded side. That's working against the gradient — that's active transport, and it cost YOU energy (just like it costs the cell ATP).
