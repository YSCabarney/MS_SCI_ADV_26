🏐 Teacher Plan · Unit: Cells

Through the Net: Cell Transport

The next step after organelles and the scale lab. Students zoom in on the cell membrane's job, "controls what enters and exits the cell," and learn how materials actually get across: passive transport (free, downhill) and active transport (costs energy, uphill).

Grade 7 Life Science 2 sessions × 40 min 3 players, virtual NGSS MS-LS1-2
Where we are

How this builds on what they know

  • Cell membrane (Word Wall card #5): they can already say it "controls what enters and exits." Now they find out how.
  • Mitochondria release energy. Active transport is where the cell spends that energy.
  • Ribosomes build proteins and the Golgi ships them. Ribosomes build the transport proteins, and the Golgi's packages leave the cell by exocytosis.
  • One Job Down, Team Down: if the membrane stops working, the whole cell fails, just like the broken rally.
  • Scale Lab: they reuse the same skills: keep the ruler in the photo, write the testable question, hypothesis and variables, and treat small measurement differences as real data.
Goals

Learning objectives

By the end of Session 2, each student can:

  1. Explain that the cell membrane is selectively permeable: it lets some materials through and blocks others.
  2. Explain the difference between passive transport (high → low concentration, no energy) and active transport (low → high, uses energy), using a real-world example of each.
  3. Describe osmosis as water moving across a membrane, and use lab evidence (potato strips and cucumber cells) to predict which way water moves.
  4. Plan and carry out a fair test and support a claim with data. This builds on the Scale Lab.
The big idea

Teacher cheat sheet

🛝 Passive transport

Materials move from high → low concentration, down the gradient. The cell spends no energy.

  • Diffusion: oxygen in, carbon dioxide out
  • Osmosis: water across the membrane
  • Some molecules need a transport protein "door" but still use no energy

Volleyball: a ball rolling downhill off a tilted court. Nobody has to push it.

🏋️ Active transport

Materials move from low → high concentration, against the gradient. Uses energy from the mitochondria.

  • Transport protein pumps, like the sodium-potassium pump in nerve and muscle cells
  • Endocytosis: the membrane wraps around and swallows big particles
  • Exocytosis: vesicles from the Golgi fuse with the membrane and dump their contents outside

Volleyball: shagging every ball back into one already-full cart. That's sweat and effort.

Watch for these misconceptions

  • "Molecules stop moving at equilibrium." No. They keep moving, but in equal amounts both ways, so nothing changes overall.
  • "Passive means nothing is happening." Lots of motion is happening. The cell just isn't paying for it.
  • "Salt moves into the potato." The big visible change is water leaving the cells, which is osmosis. Make students say where the water went.
Session 1 · 40 min

Downhill vs. Uphill

0–3
Classroom · on videoHuddle recall. Ask: "Grab Word Wall card #5. What's the cell membrane's job?" Then: "Today we find out how it decides what gets in."
3–18
Gymnasium · prop the device on a chairEngagement: Downhill / Uphill. Uses only volleyballs, the court, and a stopwatch. Round 1, The Spread: the girls push the balls around randomly and count them every 30 s, which shows diffusion and then equilibrium. Round 2, The Shag: they put every ball back into the crowded corner and compare their pulse before and after, so they feel the energy cost of active transport. Round 3, Film Room: they sort five body examples into downhill or uphill to check understanding. Each round ends with a matching animation.
18–30
Classroom tableMini-lesson (share screen). Share your screen on the Word Wall page and play its animations in this order: concentration gradient → diffusion → passive transport → osmosis → active transport → endocytosis/exocytosis. Pause each one and ask, "Which way is it moving, and is the cell paying energy?" Then ask the 3 quick checks below.
30–37
Classroom table · Google DocLab preview. Open the Salty Spuds lab. Read the testable question together. Each student writes a hypothesis (If… then… because…) and the IV, DV and 3 controlled variables, just like the Scale Lab.
37–40
ClassroomAssign homework: Transport Word Wall (10 cards). Remind them to bring cards and lab materials to Session 2.

Mini-lesson quick checks (call them out, then reveal)

1. Someone makes popcorn in the kitchen, and soon you can smell it in your bedroom. Passive or active? Why?

Passive (diffusion). The scent molecules spread from high concentration (kitchen) to low (bedroom) without anyone pushing them.

2. A root cell pulls in minerals even though there are already MORE minerals inside the cell than in the soil. Passive or active?

Active. The minerals move from low → high, against the gradient, so the cell has to spend energy (from mitochondria) running pumps.

3. Wilted lettuce gets crispy again after sitting in cold water. What moved, and which direction?

Water moved into the lettuce cells by osmosis, from where there was more water (the bowl) to where there was less (inside the cells).

Session 2 · 40 min

Salty Spuds & Sweaty Cucumbers Lab

Before class (adult on site): cut 3 potato strips the same size (about 1 cm × 1 cm × 6 cm, like thick fries) and set them aside wrapped in damp paper towel. Then cut a few thin cucumber slices. Set out 3 cups, salt, measuring spoons, the mm rulers and the charged microscopes.

0–4
Classroom tableWord Wall flash. Each player picks a card at random, reads the word, and the team gives the definition. Collect the cards for the wall.
4–9
Classroom tablePart A setup. Each player owns one cup: tap water / a little salt / a lot of salt. Measure the starting length of each strip against the ruler, do the floppy test, then drop all 3 in together. Start the 25-minute soak timer.
9–19
Classroom table · microscopesPart B: Cucumber Sweat. Salt half of a cucumber slice. Take microscope photos at 0, 3 and 6 minutes, comparing the salted and plain halves. Students watch water beads form on the salted side as cells lose water by osmosis.
19–30
Classroom table · poster boardPart C: Membrane Playbook Poster. Each player leads one zone of the poster. The data table is left blank for the results.
30–35
Classroom tablePart A results. Pull the strips out, pat them dry, measure the ending length, do the floppy test again, and fill in the data table on the poster.
35–40
Hold the poster up to the cameraFinal huddle and exit ticket. Each player answers one question out loud (below). The analysis questions and conclusion go in their Google Doc as homework.

Exit ticket: one per player

  1. Which strip got floppiest, and where did its water go?
  2. What did you see on the salted half of the cucumber, and what is the name for that process?
  3. Name one thing your body does with active transport, and which organelle pays for it.
Checklist

Materials

Session 1 · Gym

  • 10–12 volleyballs
  • A phone stopwatch
  • A device on a chair so you can see the court
  • No tape needed: the center line under the net is the "membrane"

Session 2 · Classroom table

  • Digital microscopes (charged) + Max-See app
  • 1 potato, 1 cucumber, table salt, measuring spoons
  • 3 clear cups, water, paper towels, plate
  • Millimeter rulers (from the Scale Lab)
  • Poster board + markers
  • Knife, for the adult only
Assessment

How you'll know they got it

  • Formative: the Round 1 ball-count data, the Film Room calls in Round 3, the mini-lesson quick checks, and the exit ticket.
  • Homework: the 10 Word Wall cards, with definitions in their own words and one real example each.
  • Lab: the Google Doc (hypothesis, variables, data, analysis, conclusion) and the Membrane Playbook Poster.

Adjusting the difficulty

  • Extra support: give a sentence frame: "Water moved from the ____ to the ____ because there was more ____ in the ____."
  • Extra challenge: calculate the percent change in length: (end − start) ÷ start × 100. Or predict what would happen to a strip in sugar water.
