🏐 Kickoff Activity · 15 Minutes · Gym

Downhill / Uphill

Your cell membrane decides what gets in and what gets out. Some things cross for free, and some cost the cell energy. Today you'll act out both on the court and feel the difference in your own heartbeat.

⏱ 15 min 👥 3 players 📍 Gymnasium 🏐 Volleyballs only

All you need

10–12 volleyballs, the court, and a phone stopwatch. That's it. The center line under the net is your cell membrane. Side A is the side where all the balls start, and Side B is the other side.

Ankle rule: walk, don't run, and never step on a ball.

How It Runs

  1. 0:00–1:30 Huddle. Claim your job. Prop the device up so your teacher can see the court, then start the clock.
  2. 1:30–6:30 Round 1: The Spread. What do crowded things do when nobody is in charge?
  3. 6:30–10:30 Round 2: The Shag. What does it cost to move things back into the crowd?
  4. 10:30–14:00 Round 3: Film Room. Spot the same two moves happening inside your own body.
  5. 14:00–15:00 Final huddle. Say the big idea together.

Round 1 · The Spread

≈ 5 min

The point: molecules are always moving around randomly. When a lot of them are crowded in one spot, that random motion spreads them out on its own. Nobody has to aim, and the cell doesn't have to spend any energy.

  1. Ball Boss: pile all the balls tightly into one back corner of Side A. Stat Keeper types: 0 sec: Side A 12, Side B 0.
  2. Molecule mode: all three of you walk slowly all over both sides of the court. Any time a ball is near you, give it a gentle push with your hand or the side of your foot in a random direction. No aiming, because molecules don't have a plan. Balls are allowed to roll under the net.
  3. Every 30 seconds, the Timer calls "Freeze!" The Stat Keeper counts the balls on each side and types the numbers in the chat. Then you keep going.
  4. Do 4 freezes (2 minutes total).

Talk about it: what happened to the difference between the two sides?

The big difference shrank on its own. Balls moved from the crowded side to the empty side, from high concentration to low. That's diffusion.

Once the sides were about even, did balls stop crossing the line? No. They kept crossing both ways, about equally. That balanced state is called equilibrium.

Real molecules jiggle on their own. You were just playing the part of that jiggling. The cell spends no energy on this. That's what makes it passive transport.

Round 2 · The Shag

≈ 4 min

The point: sometimes a cell needs more of something it already has a lot of. Moving things into the crowd doesn't happen by itself. It takes work, and you're about to feel how much.

  1. Pulse check #1: the Timer counts 15 seconds while everyone counts their own heartbeats (two fingers on the side of your neck). Type the numbers in the chat.
  2. Shag it: get every ball back into the crowded corner of Side A, as fast as you can. The Timer times it.Every ball you carry goes from where there are few to where there are already many. That's against the crowd, or uphill.
  3. Pulse check #2: as soon as the last ball is in, count for 15 seconds again. Multiply by 4 to get beats per minute.
Talk about it: why did your pulse go up in Round 2 and not Round 1?

In Round 1 the balls spread out downhill (crowded → empty), and the random pushing was easy. In Round 2 you forced them uphill (empty → crowded). That took effort, and your heart rate is the energy bill.

Cells do this too. It's called active transport. Cells use pump proteins in the membrane and pay with energy from their mitochondria.

Round 3 · Film Room: Your Body

≈ 3½ min

The point: Rounds 1 and 2 used volleyballs. This round checks that you can spot the same two moves happening inside your own body during practice. It also previews tonight's Word Wall words.

  1. All three of you stand on the center line.
  2. The Ball Boss reads one play out loud. On "Go!", each of you steps to Side B if you think it's DOWNHILL (passive, free) or to Side A if you think it's UPHILL (active, costs energy).
  3. If you disagree, each of you gets 10 seconds to argue your case. Then click the play to check the answer.
Play 1 · You breathe in during a timeout. Your lungs are full of oxygen, and your blood has less.

DOWNHILL: passive transport. Oxygen moves from high (lungs) to low (blood) by diffusion, with no energy needed. Small molecules like oxygen slip right through the membrane.

Play 2 · During a long rally, your muscle cells make carbon dioxide. There's more inside the cells than in your blood.

DOWNHILL: passive transport. Carbon dioxide diffuses out of the cell, from high to low. That's the same move as Play 1, just in the other direction.

Play 3 · After practice you drink a bottle of water, and the water moves into your thirsty cells.

DOWNHILL: passive transport. When water diffuses across a membrane, it has its own name: osmosis. You'll see it happen in real plant cells in the lab.

Play 4 · To get ready for your next jump, your muscle and nerve cells push sodium OUT, even though there's already way more sodium outside.

UPHILL: active transport. This moves sodium from low to high, against the crowd, using pump proteins. It's exactly like the Round 2 animation. Your body spends a big part of its energy at rest just running these pumps.

Play 5 · A white blood cell finds a germ and swallows it whole.

UPHILL: active transport. The germ is far too big for any door, so the membrane wraps around it and pinches off. That's called endocytosis, and it costs energy.

Final huddle: say it back together

"Downhill is free. Uphill costs energy."

Downhill (high → low) is passive transport. Uphill (low → high) is active transport. Next class you'll watch water slide downhill out of real plant cells under your microscope.
