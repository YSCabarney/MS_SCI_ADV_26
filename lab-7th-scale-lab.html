7th Grade Life Science · Team Lab (groups of 3)

The Scale Lab

You will use a digital microscope to measure the tiny features of everyday objects, figure out their real sizes, and use what you learn to picture how small the parts of a cell really are.

Microscope set-up: 20–30 min (first time only) Lab (Parts 2–4): 30–60 min Report: finish after the lab
Our Testable Question

Does the real size we calculate for a tiny feature stay the same when we change the microscope's magnification?

If the answer is yes, then scale works, and we can use scale to estimate the size of things that are too small to see, like the parts inside a cell. You will also observe the shapes of your objects and make educated guesses about why they are built that way.

PART 0

Set up your microscope

≈ 20–30 min · first time only

Your microscope is brand new, so take your time. Work with your teammates on your call. If a step does not work, use the Troubleshooting table at the end of this part.

Step 0A · Unbox and find the parts

  1. Open the box carefully. Take out the microscope, the stand, the cable(s) and adapter(s), and the paper manual. Keep the manual and box.

    Your box may also have extras (a case, a small ruler, a second cable). Set extras aside. If you are not sure what something is, ask your teacher.

  2. Compare your microscope to the drawing below. The names on your parts may be in different places, but every microscope like this has the same main parts.

Step 0B · Put the microscope on the stand

  1. Place the stand on a flat, steady table. Put a sheet of white paper on the base.

  2. Slide the microscope body into the round holder on the stand. The lens must point down at the base. Tighten the small knob or screw on the holder (if there is one) so the microscope stays in place but can still slide up and down when you loosen it.

  3. Press and hold the power button for 2–3 seconds. The LED lights should turn on. If nothing happens, plug the cable into your Chromebook (Step 0C) and try again.

Step 0C · Connect it to your Chromebook

Your microscope is a wireless microscope, but it also works like a webcam when you plug it into a computer with the cable. On a Chromebook, this is the easiest way. Start with Plan A.

Plan A — USB cable + the Camera app (try this first)
  1. Plug the cable into the microscope, and plug the other end into a USB port on your Chromebook. If the plug does not fit, use the small adapter that came in the box.
  2. Make sure the microscope is on (LED lights glowing).
  3. Click the circle (Launcher) in the bottom-left corner of your screen. Type Camera and open the Camera app.
  4. You will probably see your own face first. That is your Chromebook's built-in camera. Look for the Switch camera button (two curved arrows) or click the Settings gear in the Camera app, and choose the microscope. It may be named something like GENERAL-UVC or USB Camera.
  5. You should now see a bright close-up of whatever is under the lens. Move your hand under it to test.
Plan B — Use Google Meet to see the microscope (if the Camera app does not find it)
  1. With the microscope plugged in, open Google Meet in Chrome.
  2. Click the Settings (gear) icon, then Video.
  3. Under Camera, pick the microscope from the list. You should see the preview.
  4. You can share this with your team by joining a call and choosing the microscope as your camera.
Plan C — Use a phone or tablet (only if Plans A and B fail)
  1. Turn on the microscope. It makes its own Wi-Fi network. The network name and app name are in the paper manual (for example, a network named Max-see).
  2. On the phone or tablet, open Wi-Fi settings and join the microscope's network. No password is usually needed.
  3. Open the app named in the manual. Your phone will not have internet while it is connected to the microscope.
  4. Do the lab from the phone's screen, measuring the phone's screen with your ruler instead.

Step 0D · Learn the controls

  1. Brightness: turn the LED brightness wheel until the image is bright but not washed out. For this lab, once you choose a brightness, keep it the same for every photo (it is one of your controlled variables).

  2. Sharp image (focus): put a piece of paper with printed words under the lens. Slowly turn the focus wheel until the letters look crisp. Note: some microscopes have zoom buttons on the body. They usually do not work when the microscope is plugged into a computer. That is normal.

  3. How much you see: loosen the holder knob and slide the microscope up to see a wider area (smaller things), or down to zoom in closer. Tighten the knob again. Sliding the microscope up or down is how you will change the magnification in this lab.

  4. Take a photo: click the round shutter button in the Camera app. A small picture (thumbnail) appears in the corner. Click it to open the photo. Make the photo fill your screen. This is how you will measure.

    If you cannot find your photos later, open the Files app and check Downloads or a Camera folder.

PART 1

The scale method: a ruler in every photo

≈ 5–10 min

To find how big something really is, we need to know how much the microscope magnifies. That changes when you move the microscope up or down, so we measure it every time by putting a ruler in the same photo as the object.

Big idea: scale

Scale tells you how much bigger something looks in an image than it is in real life.

scale = size in the image ÷ real size

A scale of 20× means everything in the image looks 20 times bigger than it really is.

How it works

  1. Put your object and the millimeter side of your tape measure flat on the paper, side by side. Take a photo that shows both.

  2. Calibrate: pick two marks on the photo's ruler that are far apart (for example, 5 mm apart). Real distance = 5 mm. Now hold your own ruler against your screen and measure how far apart those same two marks are on the screen. That is the on-screen distance.

  3. Find the scale: scale = on-screen distance ÷ real distance.

  4. Measure your feature (like the width of one crystal) on the screen. Then real size = on-screen size ÷ scale.

  5. Change units: 1 mm = 1,000 µm (micrometers). Real size in µm = real size in mm × 1,000.

Worked example
  • The two ruler marks are 5 mm apart in real life. On my screen they are 100 mm apart.
  • Scale = 100 ÷ 5 = 20×.
  • One salt crystal measures 12 mm on my screen.
  • Real size = 12 ÷ 20 = 0.6 mm.
  • In micrometers: 0.6 × 1,000 = 600 µm.

Practice (do these before you touch the microscope again)

  1. Two marks 4 mm apart on the photo ruler measure 120 mm on your screen. What is the scale?
    Check my answer120 ÷ 4 = 30×
  2. At that scale, a thread measures 6 mm on your screen. What is its real width in mm? In µm?
    Check my answer6 ÷ 30 = 0.2 mm = 200 µm
  3. Change 0.045 mm into micrometers.
    Check my answer0.045 × 1,000 = 45 µm
PART 2

Collect your data

≈ 25–40 min
Your variables
  • Independent variable: the magnification setting (Low or High).
  • Dependent variable: the real size we calculate for a feature (in µm).
  • Controlled variables (keep these the same): the same object and the same feature, the same ruler, the same LED brightness, the same white background, and the same way of measuring on the screen.

Each teammate chooses different objects from their teammates when possible. Every object must be dry, small, flat-ish, and safe (see the safety rules). Tell your team which ones you picked.

Sanity check

Most of the features you measure will be between about 10 µm and 1,000 µm (0.01 mm to 1 mm). If your answer is much bigger or much smaller than that, re-check your measuring and your math.

Step 2C · Photograph and measure each object (repeat for all 3)

  1. Set up. Put a clean white sheet of paper on the base. Lay your object and the millimeter side of your tape measure flat on the paper, touching side by side. Set your LED brightness and do not change it again.

  2. LOW setting. Slide the microscope up until you can see about 8 to 12 millimeter marks on the ruler. Turn the focus wheel until the object and the ruler marks are sharp. Take a photo. Type in your sheet: object, feature, and Low.

  3. HIGH setting. Slide the microscope down until you can see only about 2 to 4 millimeter marks. Focus again. Take a photo. Type it in as High.

    The feature you chose must still be in the picture and sharp. If it is not, move the object.

  4. Open your Low photo full-screen. Pick two ruler marks that are far apart (5 mm is a good choice). Type 5 in column D. Hold your ruler against the screen and measure the distance between those two marks. Type it in column E.

  5. Measure your feature 3 times on the screen (three different crystals, cells, or fibers in the same photo). Type them in columns G, H, and I. The spreadsheet finds the average.

  6. Repeat steps 4–5 for the High photo. Use the ruler marks in that photo. The scale will be a different number.

  7. Reality check. Look at column L. The Low and High real sizes for the same feature should be close to each other (within about 25%). If they are far apart, re-measure. Common mistakes: measuring different features, mixing up inches and millimeters, or forgetting to use the ruler marks from the same photo.

Measuring tips
  • Measure to the nearest half millimeter on your screen. Line the ruler's edge up carefully with the left edge of your feature.
  • Keep your eye straight above the screen so the ruler does not look shifted.
  • Your photos may look different sizes on the screen from one photo to the next. That is fine. Each photo has its own ruler, so each photo has its own scale.
  • Paste at least one Low photo for each object into your Lab Report (Insert → Image → Upload from computer).

PART 3

Observe shapes and structure

≈ 5–10 min

You are also a scientist who asks, "Why is it built that way?" Use your photos (and look again under the microscope) to make observations. Record them in the Shape & Structure table in your Lab Report.

  1. Describe the shapes. Circles? Rectangles? Cubes? Tubes? Threads crossing each other? Overlapping scales? Is a shape repeated over and over?

  2. Notice the size and pattern. Are all the pieces about the same size, or do they vary? Are they packed tightly or spread out?

  3. Make an educated guess. What job might that shape do? Use the clue bank below. There is no single right answer. You must explain your reasoning.

If you see…It might…
Long, thin tubes or fiberscarry liquids, add strength, or trap and absorb water
Boxes or rectangles stacked tightlyfill space with no gaps, hold a firm shape, support weight
Overlapping flat scales or platesprotect, shed water, or lock together
Threads crossing over and underbe strong but flexible
Flat, straight sides and sharp corners (like cubes)show how particles pack in a regular pattern
Lots of tiny holeslet air, water, or light pass through, or hold liquid
Connect to cells

Cells work the same way. A plant cell's cell wall is stiff and box-shaped so plant cells stack together and hold a plant up. What might a cell's shape tell you about its job?

PART 4

Graph your data

≈ 10–15 min

Your team makes one column chart that compares the Low and High real sizes for every object.

Open the Graphing Guide (Example 1)

  1. Make sure your Team Summary table has these columns: Object, Low setting real size (µm), High setting real size (µm). (You do not need to include the % difference column in the chart.)

  2. Highlight the Object, Low, and High columns, including the headings. Click Insert → Chart.

  3. In the Chart editor, choose Column chart. You should see two colored columns for each object.

  4. Under Customize, add a chart title (example: Calculated Real Size of Features at Low and High Magnification), a horizontal axis title (Object), and a vertical axis title (Real size (µm)). Make sure the legend is on.

  5. Copy the chart and paste it (unlinked) into your Lab Report. Write a caption that says what the chart shows.

How to read your chart

If scale really works, the two columns for each object should be almost the same height. That would be evidence for your hypothesis. If some are very different, think about why (see the discussion questions).

PART 5

Scale calculations: how big are cell parts?

≈ 15–20 min · no microscope needed

You cannot see inside a cell with this microscope, but you can use scale to picture cell parts. Here is a drawing of a plant cell. The drawn sizes are on the left of each label and the real sizes are on the right. Real sizes are typical, approximate values.

Units reminder

1 cm = 10 mm · 1 mm = 1,000 µm · so 1 cm = 10,000 µm.

Worked example: a drawing is 10 cm wide and the real cell is 100 µm wide. Change 10 cm into µm: 10 × 10,000 = 100,000 µm. Scale = 100,000 ÷ 100 = 1,000×.

Answer these in your Lab Report (show your work)

  1. Scale of the drawing. Use the nucleus (drawn 1 cm, real 10 µm) to calculate the scale. Does it match the scale from the whole cell?
  2. Predict a drawing. A typical animal cheek cell is about 20 µm wide. At the same scale as this drawing, how wide would you draw it (in cm)?
  3. Check the mitochondrion. Use the scale to show that a 1 µm mitochondrion is drawn about 1 mm long.
  4. Your microscope's view. Look at the scale you calculated for your High setting (column F in your Sheet). At that scale, how big would a 10 µm nucleus look on your screen (in mm)? A 1 µm mitochondrion? A 0.02 µm ribosome?
  5. Can we see it? The smallest thing you can clearly see on a screen is about 0.2 mm. Using question 4, decide which cell parts your microscope could show you and which it could not. Explain.
  6. Classroom-size cell. Imagine building a model of a plant cell that is 5 m wide (about the size of a small classroom). Calculate the scale (5 m = 5,000,000 µm). How big would the nucleus, a chloroplast, and a mitochondrion be in the model? Give each answer in a sensible unit (cm or mm).
  7. Compare to your data. Pick one of your objects. About how many of its measured features would fit across one 100 µm plant cell?

GLOSSARY

Words to know

WordMeaning
MagnificationHow much bigger something looks in an image compared to real life.
ScaleThe ratio of the image size to the real size. Scale = image size ÷ real size.
FeatureA small part of an object you choose to measure, like one crystal or one fiber.
Millimeter (mm)One thousandth of a meter. About the thickness of a credit card edge.
Micrometer (µm)One thousandth of a millimeter. 1 mm = 1,000 µm. A human hair is about 70 µm wide.
Structure and functionThe way something is built (structure) is related to the job it does (function).
Independent variableWhat you change on purpose.
Dependent variableWhat you measure to see the effect.
Controlled variableSomething you keep the same so the test is fair.
Percent differenceHow far apart two numbers are, compared to their average, shown as a percent.
