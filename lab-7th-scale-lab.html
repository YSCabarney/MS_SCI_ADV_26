Before You Start

How to Use Your Microscope

Your microscope is a small handheld WiFi digital microscope — not a lab microscope on a stand. It connects wirelessly to an app on a phone or tablet, and it only focuses clearly when it's held very close to whatever you're looking at.

Your exact microscope — button positions may vary slightly from this drawing, but every part shown here is on your model.
  1. Charge it. Plug the USB-C port into a charger or computer until it's full before lab day.
  2. Install the app. On the phone or tablet you'll use, install the free "Max-See" app from your app store — do this ahead of time, not during the lab.
  3. Power on. Long-press the power button until the LED ring lights turn on.
  4. Connect the WiFi. On your phone/tablet, open WiFi settings and connect directly to the microscope's own signal (something like "Max-see"). This is not your home WiFi — the microscope broadcasts its own signal.
  5. Open the app. Open Max-See. You should see a live close-up video feed — that's your microscope's camera.
  6. Get in range. This microscope only focuses clearly when the lens is about 3–60 millimeters from your object — roughly a fingertip's width to a couple inches. Too close or too far and everything blurs.
💡

Pro tip: Rest your elbow flat on the table while holding the microscope, or use the included stand — even small hand shake looks huge at this range.

ProblemTry this
App won't find the microscope's WiFi signalTurn the microscope off and back on. Make sure your phone's WiFi is on and searching for new networks.
Image is blurry no matter whatYou're likely outside the 3–60mm range. Slowly move closer or farther until it snaps into focus.
Image is too dark or washed outAdjust the brightness setting in the app, or change the angle of your object slightly.
App keeps disconnectingMove your phone closer to the microscope — WiFi range is short. Restart the app if needed.
Objective

Why This Lab Matters

You've learned that scientists calculate the true size of things they discover, no matter how much they've had to zoom in to see them. Today you'll design and run your own controlled experiment to test whether that's actually true for your own microscope — using nothing but a piece of food and a plain millimeter ruler.

Step 1

Background Research

Before you touch the microscope, research and write one paragraph (4–6 sentences) in your Google Doc that covers:

A definition of magnification, in your own words.
Why scientists need a reference object of known size (like a ruler) to find the TRUE size of something viewed through a magnifying tool.
One real-world example of why knowing an object's actual size — not just how big it looks — matters in science.
Step 2

Testable Question

"Does the real size we calculate for a tiny feature stay the same when we change the microscope's magnification?"

Step 3

Hypothesis

In your Google Doc, write your hypothesis as an If …, then …, because … sentence:

"If I change the microscope's magnification, then the calculated real size of my tiny feature will ______, because ______."

💡 Need a hint?
Think about what magnification actually does — does it change how big something looks, or how big it actually is?
Step 4

Variables

In your Google Doc, identify the independent variable, dependent variable, and at least three controlled variables for this exact experiment.

💡 Hint: Independent Variable
This is the one thing you're going to change on purpose, on purpose, between your three trials — think about what changes on your microscope's app.
💡 Hint: Dependent Variable
This is the number you calculate and record every trial — the thing you're watching to see if it changes.
💡 Hint: Controlled Variables
What has to stay exactly the same across all three trials for this to be a fair test? Think about the object, the feature, the ruler, and the lighting.

Step 6

Safety First

⚠️ Everyone follows these
  • Wash your hands before and after this lab.
  • Do not eat any food that has touched the microscope, the ruler, or been sitting out during the lab.
  • Keep the microscope — an electronic device — away from wet or gooey food; don't let food touch the lens.
  • Handle the ruler carefully.
  • Dispose of your food specimen in the trash when you're finished — don't leave it out.
Step 7

Procedure

Correct setup — food and ruler must both appear in the same photo, every time.
  1. Choose a food that is solid or gooey — not a liquid. Check with an adult before using it.
  2. Place a small piece of your food on a plate or napkin.
  3. Look closely at your food and pick ONE tiny feature that interests you — a seed, an air bubble, a bump, a fiber, a grain, a crystal, a pore, etc.
  4. Place your millimeter ruler directly against your tiny feature so both will appear in the same shot.
  5. Hold the lens over your food and ruler at a LOW zoom setting until the image is sharp. Take a photo.
  6. Without moving your food or ruler, zoom in to a MEDIUM setting on the same tiny feature. Refocus if needed. Take a photo.
  7. Zoom in further to a HIGH setting on the same tiny feature. Refocus if needed. Take a photo.
  8. For each of your 3 photos, count how many millimeter marks on the ruler your tiny feature spans. Record that number as your measured size in your data table.
💡

Pro tip: Pick a feature with clear, sharp edges — a fuzzy or see-through edge is much harder to measure accurately, and that makes your data noisier.

💡

Pro tip: Don't nudge the food or the ruler between shots. If either one moves, that trial isn't a fair comparison anymore.

Step 9

Analysis Questions

Answer these in complete sentences in your Google Doc:

  1. Look at your three measurements. Did the size you calculated for your tiny feature stay about the same across all three zoom levels, or did it change? Use your data table as evidence.
  2. If your measurements were not exactly identical, what real, specific reasons might explain the small differences?
  3. Why does keeping the ruler in the very same photo as your tiny feature let you calculate its true size, no matter what zoom level you use?
  4. Was your hypothesis supported by your data? Explain using your actual results.
  5. How does this connect to what real scientists have to do any time they use a microscope — even a very powerful one — to measure something they've discovered?
Step 10

Conclusion

In one paragraph in your Google Doc: restate the testable question in your own words, state what your data showed, explain whether your hypothesis was supported, and mention at least one real source of error in your measurements.

Reference

Glossary

MagnificationHow much bigger something looks in an image compared to real life.
Real sizeAn object's actual size, no matter how big or small it looks under magnification.
FeatureA small part of an object you choose to measure — like one seed or one bubble.
Millimeter (mm)One thousandth of a meter — about the thickness of a credit card's edge.
Micrometer (µm)One thousandth of a millimeter. 1 mm = 1,000 µm.
Independent variableWhat you change on purpose.
Dependent variableWhat you measure to see the effect.
Controlled variableSomething you keep the same so the test is fair.
ResolutionHow much real, sharp detail an image actually shows — different from how big it looks.
