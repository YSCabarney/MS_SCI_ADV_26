01

What Is Cell Theory?

Cell theory is one of the foundational ideas in all of biology. It's not a guess — it's a theory built from decades of observations by multiple scientists, tested and confirmed again and again with better microscopes and better methods. In science, a theory isn't a hunch; it's an explanation backed by huge amounts of evidence.

  1. All living things are made of one or more cells. Whether it's a single bacterium or a 6-foot-tall volleyball player, every organism is built from cells.
  2. The cell is the basic unit of structure and function in living things. The cell is the smallest unit that can still be considered "alive" and carry out life's basic jobs.
  3. All cells come from pre-existing cells. Cells don't just appear out of nowhere — they're produced when existing cells divide.
Timeout 1Which of these is NOT one of the three parts of cell theory?
A) All living things are made of cells   B) All cells come from pre-existing cells   C) All cells contain chlorophyll   D) The cell is the basic unit of life

Answer: C. Chlorophyll is only found in plant cells (and some other producers) — it's not part of every cell, so it can't be part of the theory that applies to all living things.

02

Who Built This Theory?

Cell theory wasn't discovered in one day by one person — it was built over 190 years by scientists improving each other's work, just like a team builds a winning system season after season.

1665

Robert Hooke

Looked at a thin slice of cork under a basic microscope and saw tiny empty boxes. He named them "cells" because they reminded him of the small rooms (cells) monks lived in.

1670s

Anton van Leeuwenhoek

Built more powerful microscopes and was the first person to observe living cells — including bacteria and cells swimming in pond water.

1838

Matthias Schleiden

A botanist who concluded that all plants are made of cells.

1839

Theodor Schwann

A zoologist who concluded that all animals are made of cells too — combined with Schleiden's work, this gave us tenet #1.

1855

Rudolf Virchow

Added the final piece: cells only come from other cells through division — they don't spontaneously appear. This completed the theory.

Timeout 2Which scientist proved that all cells come from other pre-existing cells, and why did this matter for the theory?

Rudolf Virchow (1855). Before this, some people believed living things could pop into existence from non-living matter ("spontaneous generation"). Virchow's idea — that cells always come from other cells — completed cell theory and ruled that possibility out.

03

Every Team Needs Positions

A cell isn't just one blob doing everything — it's more like a volleyball team. Inside every cell are tiny working structures called organelles ("little organs"), and just like players on a roster, each one has a specific job that keeps the whole team — the cell — running. Here's the starting lineup:

One more thing to notice as you go: the shape and size of each organelle below is never random. Every fold, every curve, every stack exists because that exact shape helps the organelle do its job better — the same way a libero's low stance and a hitter's vertical jump are shaped by exactly what their position needs them to do.

Head Coach

Nucleus

Contains the cell's DNA and controls almost everything the cell does.

Shape matters: Its round shape with small pores keeps the DNA sealed and protected, while still letting specific instructions pass in and out.

On the team: The coach who holds the playbook, calls the plays, and directs every other position on the court.

Fun fact: mature red blood cells are one of the only human cells with no nucleus — see the Real Teams section below!

Gatekeeper

Cell Membrane

A flexible outer boundary that controls what enters and exits the cell.

Shape matters: Its thin, flexible double layer can bend, stretch, and fold — allowing it to control traffic in and out while still adapting to the cell's shape.

On the team: The check-in table at the gym doors — it decides who's allowed on the court and what gear can come in or out.

It's "selectively permeable" — picky about what it lets through, not a solid wall.

The Court

Cytoplasm

The jelly-like fluid that fills the cell and holds all the organelles in place.

Shape matters: It has no fixed shape of its own — being a fluid is exactly what lets it fill every gap and hold organelles wherever they're needed.

On the team: The gym floor itself — the space where every play actually happens.

Most of a cell's chemical reactions happen right in the cytoplasm.

Conditioning Coach

Mitochondria

Breaks down nutrients to release usable energy (ATP) for the cell — the "powerhouse of the cell."

Shape matters: Its inner membrane is folded into ridges called cristae. More folds mean more surface area — and more surface area means more energy made in the same small space.

On the team: The strength & conditioning coach who fuels every player so they have the energy to hit, dig, and jump.

Muscle cells — the ones athletes rely on most — are packed with extra mitochondria.

Playmakers

Ribosomes

Build proteins, the molecules that do most of the actual work in a cell.

Shape matters: They're tiny and round, so thousands of them can be packed into the cytoplasm — or ride along the ER — without taking up much room at all.

On the team: The players actually running each play and making things happen on the court.

Some ribosomes float free in the cytoplasm; others are attached to the ER, like starters vs. bench players.

Hallways

Endoplasmic Reticulum

A network of pathways that moves proteins and other materials around inside the cell.

Shape matters: Its folded, maze-like shape packs a huge amount of surface area into a small space — more surface means more room to build and move material at once.

On the team: The hallways connecting the locker room, gym, and equipment room — the routes everything travels through.

"Rough" ER is covered in ribosomes (building materials in transit); "smooth" ER has none.

Equipment Manager

Golgi Apparatus

Packages, labels, and ships proteins to wherever they're needed, inside or outside the cell.

Shape matters: Its stacked, flattened layers let a protein move through one sac at a time, getting finished a little more at each stage — an assembly line built right into its shape.

On the team: The equipment manager packing team gear into bags and shipping them to the right place for game day.

Think of it as the cell's post office.

Storage Closet

Vacuole

A storage sac for water, nutrients, and waste.

Shape matters: It's simple and roomy on purpose — a plain sac shape maximizes how much it can hold, which is the whole point of storage.

On the team: The equipment closet storing water bottles, spare balls, and extra jerseys.

Plant cells have one giant vacuole; animal cells have several small ones.

Clean-Up Crew

Lysosome

Contains enzymes that break down waste and worn-out cell parts.

Shape matters: It's small and round so it can travel anywhere in the cell and merge directly with whatever waste needs cleaning up.

On the team: The crew that sweeps the gym and clears out broken equipment after practice.

Found mainly in animal cells.

Timeout 3A libero is a defensive specialist who never scores by herself, but the whole system depends on the space she covers. Which organelle above works the same way — providing the "space" where everything else happens rather than doing one specific job?

Cytoplasm. It doesn't build proteins or make energy itself, but it's the fluid environment every other organelle depends on to function — just like the court itself is the space every play depends on.

Timeout 4Explain the relationship between the endoplasmic reticulum and the Golgi apparatus using a "shipping" analogy.

The ER is like the hallway/loading dock where proteins are built and start moving. The Golgi apparatus is like the shipping department — it receives what the ER sends over, packages it, labels it, and ships it to its final destination in or out of the cell.

Timeout 5Pick any two organelles above. For each one, explain in your own words how its shape actually helps it do its job — not just what its job is.

Any reasonable pairing works here — for example, the mitochondria's folded inner membrane creates more surface area for making energy, and the nucleus's round, pored shape protects the DNA while still allowing controlled traffic in and out. The goal is connecting shape to function, not just restating the function.

04

Two Different Rosters: Plant vs. Animal Cells

Not every team runs the exact same lineup. Animal cells (like yours) and plant cells share most of the same organelles above, but plant cells carry three extra pieces of equipment that change their shape and give them different jobs:

The Stadium Walls

Cell Wall

A rigid layer outside the cell membrane that gives the cell a fixed, boxy shape and extra protection.

Shape matters: Its stiff, rectangular structure is what lets plant cells stack together like bricks to hold a whole plant upright — something a flexible animal cell membrane alone can't do.

Found in plant cells only — it's also why plant cells always look boxy under a microscope while animal cells look round and irregular.

The Fuel Station

Chloroplast

Captures sunlight and uses it to make sugar (food) for the plant through photosynthesis.

Shape matters: Its flattened, layered shape (like a tiny stack of pancakes) packs in as much light-catching surface as possible in a small space.

Found in plant cells only — animal cells have no way to make their own food from sunlight, so they don't have this organelle at all.

The Mega Storage Closet

Large Central Vacuole

One giant, permanent vacuole that can take up most of the interior of a plant cell.

Shape matters: Because it's so large, it can fill with water and push outward against the cell wall — that pressure is literally what keeps stems and leaves stiff instead of wilting.

Animal cells have several small vacuoles instead of one giant one — that's why a wilted plant and a healthy one look so different.

05

Real Teams, Real Cells

Cell structure isn't just a diagram in a textbook — it explains real things happening in your own body right now.

🩸

Red blood cells ditch their nucleus

Mature red blood cells lose their nucleus as they develop — the trade-off is they can't divide anymore, but it frees up extra space to carry more oxygen-grabbing hemoglobin.

💪

Muscle cells are loaded with mitochondria

Because muscle cells need huge amounts of energy to contract, they're packed with far more mitochondria than most other cells — the same reason conditioning work pays off on the court.

🧠

Neurons stretch to fit their job

Nerve cells can be over a meter long in the human body — their shape is stretched out specifically to carry signals long distances, showing how structure follows function.

💊

Antibiotics target structures human cells don't have

Many antibiotics work by attacking the cell wall of bacteria. Human cells don't have a cell wall, so the medicine can destroy the bacteria without destroying your own cells.

🧴

Peeling skin after a sunburn

That's dead skin cells shedding so your body can replace them with new ones — a visible, everyday reminder that cells are constantly dividing and being replaced.

Timeout 6Why don't mature red blood cells have a nucleus, and what's the trade-off?

Losing the nucleus makes room for more hemoglobin, so the cell can carry more oxygen. The trade-off is that without a nucleus (and its DNA), the cell can no longer divide or repair itself — it eventually wears out and is replaced.

Timeout 7 · Variables CheckA scientist wants to test whether more sunlight makes plant cells produce more chloroplasts. Identify the independent and dependent variable.

Independent variable (what's changed on purpose): amount of sunlight. Dependent variable (what's measured/responds): number of chloroplasts produced. This is the same variable-identification skill from your last unit — it applies just as much to cell biology as anything else!

06

Breaking News: The Game Isn't Over

Cell theory was "finished" in 1855 — but that doesn't mean scientists know everything about cells. New tools keep revealing new plays.

2024–2025

🔬 Scientists discovered a brand-new organelle: the hemifusome

Researchers at the University of Virginia and the National Institutes of Health used an imaging technique that flash-freezes cells to spot a structure never described before — nicknamed the "hemifusome" because it looks like a snowman. It appears to help cells sort, recycle, and dispose of materials, and may be connected to diseases like Alzheimer's.

Read more: UVA School of Medicine →
Ongoing

🧬 Doctors are now editing cells to treat disease

A gene-editing therapy approved in the U.S. edits a patient's own bone marrow cells to treat sickle cell disease — changing the DNA inside the nucleus of living cells to fix a genetic problem at its source.

Ongoing

🧫 "Mini-organs" grown from stem cells

Labs are growing tiny, simplified versions of organs (called organoids) from stem cells to study diseases and test medicines — all starting from the basic idea that cells can be directed to grow, divide, and organize into tissue.

Timeout 8Why does the discovery of the hemifusome show that cell theory is still being explored today, even though the theory itself was completed in 1855?

Cell theory (that all living things are made of cells, cells are the basic unit of life, and cells come from other cells) is still considered true and hasn't changed. But how cells work and what's inside them is still being discovered — better tools like cryo-electron imaging let scientists see structures that were invisible before. Science keeps building on itself, the same way this discovery builds on Hooke's original 1665 observation.

Timeout 9If you were designing an experiment to study the hemifusome, what might your independent and dependent variables be?

Answers will vary — this is a discussion question! Example: independent variable = presence/absence of the hemifusome in a cell (or a chemical that blocks it from forming); dependent variable = how well the cell recycles/disposes of waste material. Any reasonable, testable pair is correct.
