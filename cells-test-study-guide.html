Cell Theory & the Scientists

A scientific theory is not a guess. It's an explanation backed by huge amounts of evidence, tested again and again.

The three parts of cell theory

  1. All living things are made of one or more cells.
  2. The cell is the basic unit of structure and function in living things. It is the smallest unit that is still "alive."
  3. All cells come from pre-existing cells, made when existing cells divide.
⚠️ Watch out: statements like "all cells have a cell wall" or "all cells have chloroplasts" are not part of cell theory. Cell theory only covers things that are true for every living thing.

Who built the theory? (about 190 years of teamwork)

  • 1665Robert Hooke looked at thin cork and saw tiny empty boxes. He named them "cells" because they reminded him of the small rooms monks lived in.
  • 1670sAnton van Leeuwenhoek built stronger microscopes and was the first to see living cells (bacteria in pond water).
  • 1838Matthias Schleiden (botanist) concluded that all plants are made of cells.
  • 1839Theodor Schwann (zoologist) concluded that all animals are made of cells.
  • 1855Rudolf Virchow showed that cells only come from other cells. This completed the theory and ruled out spontaneous generation (life popping out of non-living matter).
🔬 The game isn't over: in 2024–2025 scientists found a brand-new organelle, the hemifusome, using a technique that flash-freezes cells. It appears to help cells sort, recycle, and get rid of materials. Cell theory is still true; better tools just keep showing us more of what's inside cells.

Organelles: Shape = Job

Organelles are the "little organs" inside a cell. Know each one's job AND how its shape helps it do that job. This is called the structure–function relationship.

Head Coach

Nucleus

Holds the cell's DNA and controls almost everything the cell does.

Shape: round with small pores, so the DNA stays protected while instructions pass in and out.

Gatekeeper

Cell Membrane

Flexible outer boundary that controls what enters and exits. It is selectively permeable, meaning picky about what gets through.

Shape: thin, flexible double layer that can bend and stretch.

The Court

Cytoplasm

Jelly-like fluid that fills the cell and holds organelles in place. Most chemical reactions happen here.

Shape: no fixed shape. Being a fluid lets it fill every gap.

Conditioning Coach

Mitochondria

Breaks down nutrients to release energy as ATP. The "powerhouse of the cell."

Shape: inner membrane folded into cristae. More folds mean more surface area, which means more ATP.

Playmakers

Ribosomes

Build proteins. Some float free in the cytoplasm; others sit on the ER.

Shape: tiny and round, so thousands fit in one cell.

Hallways

Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER)

Network of pathways that moves proteins and materials. Rough ER has ribosomes; smooth ER has none (it makes lipids).

Shape: folded maze, which packs a lot of surface area into a small space.

Equipment Manager

Golgi Apparatus

Packages, labels, and ships proteins. It's the cell's post office.

Shape: stacked, flattened sacs that work like an assembly line.

Storage Closet

Vacuole

Stores water, nutrients, and waste. Animal cells have several small ones.

Shape: a simple, roomy sac, which is perfect for storage.

Clean-Up Crew

Lysosome

Uses enzymes to break down waste and worn-out cell parts. Found mainly in animal cells.

Shape: small and round, so it can travel anywhere and merge with waste.

🔁 ER + Golgi team-up: proteins are built by ribosomes on the ER and travel through its hallways, then the Golgi receives them, packages them, labels them, and ships them out.
3

Plant vs. Animal Cells

Plant and animal cells share most organelles, but plant cells carry three extras.

Stadium Walls

Cell Wall

Rigid layer outside the cell membrane, made of cellulose. Gives support and protection.

Shape: stiff and rectangular, so cells stack like bricks to hold the plant upright. It's why plant cells look boxy.

Fuel Station

Chloroplast

Captures energy from sunlight so the plant can make its own food. Animal cells don't have them.

Shape: flat, layered stacks like pancakes, which give more surface to catch light.

Mega Storage

Large Central Vacuole

One giant vacuole that fills most of the cell.

Shape: so big that when it fills with water it pushes against the cell wall and keeps stems stiff. When it loses water, the plant wilts.

Real cells, real life

  • 🩸 Red blood cells lose their nucleus. That frees up room for more hemoglobin to carry oxygen, but the trade-off is that they can't divide or repair themselves.
  • 💪 Muscle cells are packed with extra mitochondria because contracting takes lots of energy.
  • 🧠 Neurons can be over a meter long, a shape stretched to carry signals far.
  • 💊 Antibiotics often attack bacteria's cell wall. Human cells don't have one, so we're safe.
  • 🧴 Peeling skin after a sunburn is dead cells being replaced by new ones.

Cell Size & Scale

Cells and organelles are measured in micrometers (µm). 1 mm = 1,000 µm and 1 cm = 10,000 µm.

The size ladder (smallest → largest)

Ribosome
0.02 µm
Mitochondrion
1 µm
Bacterium (E. coli)
2 µm
Red blood cell
7.5 µm
Human hair (width)
70 µm
Plant cell
100 µm
Grain of salt
500 µm

Bars use a log scale: each step is about 10× bigger. A grain of salt is about 25,000× wider than a ribosome!

Using scale

scale = model size ÷ real sizeConvert both sizes to the SAME unit first!
Worked example: A model of a 100 µm cell is built 1 m wide. How big is the scale?
1 m = 1,000,000 µm → 1,000,000 ÷ 100 = 10,000×.
At that scale, a 5 µm chloroplast becomes 5 × 10,000 = 50,000 µm = 5 cm, about the size of a golf ball.

Why does a cell have one nucleus but thousands of ribosomes? Ribosomes are tiny, so lots of them fit, and the cell needs lots of proteins built. Size and job go together!

📝 Vocabulary to Know

Cell theory: the 3 ideas that explain cells in all living things
Organelle: a "little organ" with a specific job inside a cell
Selectively permeable: lets some things through, but not others
Spontaneous generation: the old, wrong idea that life comes from non-living matter
Structure–function: an organelle's shape matches its job
ATP: usable energy that mitochondria release for the cell
Cristae: folds of the mitochondria's inner membrane
Cellulose: the material a plant cell wall is made of
Reactants / Products: what goes IN to a reaction / what comes OUT
Scale: model size ÷ real size
Micrometer (µm): one-thousandth of a millimeter

✍️ Writing Strong Answers

You'll have two written-response questions. Your teacher grades these, and full explanations earn full points.

  • Answer every part. If it asks where, what it uses, and what it makes, write all three.
  • Connect shape to job. Don't just say "mitochondria make energy." Say "its folded cristae give more surface area, so it can make more ATP."
  • Use your vocab words: organelle, selectively permeable, cristae, structure–function.
  • Variables refresher: the independent variable is what you change on purpose; the dependent variable is what you measure.

🎯 Practice: Test Yourself

Answer in your head or on paper first, then tap to check.

1. Which part of cell theory explains why a cut on your skin can heal?

All cells come from pre-existing cells. Your existing skin cells divide to make new ones.

2. What did Schleiden and Schwann each conclude?

Schleiden: all plants are made of cells. Schwann: all animals are made of cells.

3. A cell is making lots of proteins to ship outside the cell. Name three organelles involved, in order.

Ribosomes (build the proteins) → endoplasmic reticulum (moves them) → Golgi apparatus (packages and ships them).

4. Why is the chloroplast shaped like a stack of flat pancakes?

The flat, layered shape packs in as much light-catching surface as possible in a small space.

5. You see a cell under a microscope. It is boxy and has green structures. Plant or animal? How do you know?

Plant. The boxy shape comes from a cell wall, and the green structures are chloroplasts. Neither is in animal cells.

6. A 10 µm nucleus is drawn at a scale of 500×. How wide is the drawing, in mm?

10 × 500 = 5,000 µm = 5 mm.

7. A student tests whether the temperature of water changes how fast a plant cell's vacuole fills. Name the independent and dependent variables.

Independent: water temperature (changed on purpose). Dependent: how fast the vacuole fills (measured).
